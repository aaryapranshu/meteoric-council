# 07 — Retention, Churn & Lifetime Value

**When to open this file:** the founder mentions churn, cancellations, refunds after onboarding, clients not renewing, "we sign people but revenue is flat", low LTV, a leaky bucket, onboarding, community, exit interviews, customer referrals, or asks how much they can afford to spend to acquire a customer.
**Route elsewhere:** designing upsells, downsells, continuity offers and pricing tests (the Crazy 8 monetization levers) → `03-money-models.md`. Post-sale cold feet within the first 24–48 hours (Reinforce) → `05-sales-and-closing.md`. Testimonial capture as a marketing system → proof cards in `05`.

## Churn Math (get the number right first)
**Use when:** any churn/retention question; the founder quotes a churn number; you need LTV.
**Core idea:** Churn = customers lost in a period ÷ customers at the *start* of that period. New sign-ups never change it. In a recurring business, lifetime ≈ 1 ÷ churn, so small changes in a small number create huge LTV swings.
**How to run it:**
1. Pick a period (monthly for subscriptions). Count customers at start (S) and how many of *those* are gone at the end (L). Churn = L ÷ S.
2. Average lifetime (periods) ≈ 1 ÷ churn. Lifetime revenue ≈ price ÷ churn. LTGP ≈ gross profit per period ÷ churn.
3. Track joins vs cancels monthly and label the business honestly: growing (joins > cancels), flat (equal), declining (fewer joins than cancels).
4. Cut churn by cohort, not just the average: month 1, months 1–3, after day 90, after month 6. Early months always run higher.
5. Don't overhaul the business because month-one churn is high if the program is under 90 days old — the average is inflated until cohorts mature. Do fix it if almost everyone leaves.
**Numbers & thresholds:**
- 100 customers → 95 next month = 5% churn; 95% retention (Retention and LTV playbooks).
- 10% → 3% monthly churn: lifetime goes from 10 to ~33 months, ~3.3x LTV; $100/mo goes from $1,000 to $3,333 LTV (Retention playbook).
- 10% → 5%: LTV doubles (LTV playbook). 20% → 10%: LTV doubles (YT 2026).
- At 15% monthly churn a gym loses ~83% of members in a year; with 250 members it needs ~208 new ones just to stay flat (Retention playbook, gym example).
- Skool benchmarks (time-bound, YT 2026; best fit for online/consumer services, not insurance or alarm contracts): platform average ~80% monthly retention (20% churn); best groups <10% churn; weak groups 30%+ churn (<70% retention). Months 1–3 run 20%+; after day 90 roughly halves to ~10% or less; by month 6 ~2%.
- He says he has never seen a business with under 3% monthly churn that doesn't make great money (Retention playbook).
**Worked example (illustrative):** Agency with 40 retainer clients loses 4 this month and signs 6. Churn is 4 ÷ 40 = 10% (not "net +2, so fine"). Average life ~10 months. Halving churn to 5% doubles every client's lifetime value with zero new marketing.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Counting new sign-ups in the churn denominator or netting them off — "People get this twisted."
- Treating churn as an action item. It's a lagging indicator; you can't "do" lower churn — you change leading indicators (activation, usage, time from sale to onboarding).
- Comparing your churn to a different business model (an annual alarm contract vs a monthly coaching group).
**Ask the founder:** How many customers did you have on the 1st of last month, and how many of *those* are still paying? What's churn for month 1 vs month 4+? What's your gross profit per customer per month?

## Why Retention Is the Highest-Leverage Lever
**Use when:** founder wants more leads but has meaningful churn; deciding where to invest next; "is retention worth the effort?"
**Core idea:** Existing customers already trust you, already got value, already gave you their card and cost nothing to acquire. A leaky bucket business must keep buying customers just to stay the same size. Keep them longer → LTV rises → you can outspend competitors for attention.
**How to run it:**
1. Compute what one point of churn is worth in LTV (see Churn Math) and compare it to the cost of the same lift through acquisition.
2. Set a retention budget: what you'll spend *extra* to keep a customer. Start at ~1/5 of CAC.
3. Model compounding: if referrals from customers outpace churn, the base grows without any other acquisition.
4. If monthly retention is below ~70%, work on retention before anything else (YT 2026).
**Numbers & thresholds:** Stats he repeats from outside sources: acquiring a customer costs 5–25x more than retaining one; a Harvard Business Review study tied a 5% retention increase to 25–95% more profit (cited in Retention playbook — third-party figures, not his data).
**Worked example (from source):** 10% churn with 10% of customers referring each month → flat. Lower churn *or* raise referrals → growth. The same activities (making customers happier) drive both.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- "Sell two to replace one" thinking — it works until it doesn't, and it accelerates how fast the market learns you're bad.
- Structural churn excuse: serving very small businesses means some go out of business — that's real, but still reduce it (Shopify and Skool serve tiny customers and are large).
> "If referrals are greater than churn, you never stop growing." — $100M Playbook: Retention
**Ask the founder:** What's your CAC and what % of it would you spend to save a customer? How many new customers came from existing customers last month vs how many left?
**Pairs with:** Referrals From Customers · LTGP & CAC Tolerance

## Why People Leave: Price-to-Value Gap and the Look-Back Window
**Use when:** "why are they cancelling?"; "it costs too much" complaints; volatile results month to month (agencies, performance services).
**Core idea:** Customers cancel at the point perceived value drops below price. Keep value above price by adding value (preferred) or lowering price. They judge on the *most recent* billing period, not the lifetime — so bill less often to lengthen the window in which value can exceed price.
**How to run it:**
1. Invert: "What would I do to lose 100% of my customers?" → ignore them, break promises, miscommunicate, treat them poorly, set unrealistic expectations, hide progress, keep them away from other happy customers, make it hard to use. Then do the opposite of each.
2. Audit each item against what actually happens in your delivery today.
3. Shift billing toward longer periods (annual, quarterly or upfront) so a bad month doesn't trigger a cancel. See Annual & Upfront Billing.
4. Sell only promises you deliver: the reason someone buys isn't always the reason they stay — know both.
**Worked example (from source, agency):** Client pays $5k/mo. Month 1 they make $40k; month 2 they make $5k; month 3 they cancel — the owner thinks "I covered eight months in month one", the client only sees "last month was break-even". Billed annually, the same year looks like $100k made vs $60k paid.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Dismissing "too expensive" as "they don't get the value" without checking for a pattern.
- Trying to sell recurring for something with one-time value (mismatch of business model and pricing model).
> "The reason someone buys isn't always necessarily the reason someone stays." — YT "If I Wanted To Scale A Service Business In 2026, Here's What I'd Do" (2026)
**Ask the founder:** What did your last 10 cancels say? How often do you bill? Do clients see their results each month, or only when they complain?
**Pairs with:** Overwhelm · Exit Interviews · Annual & Upfront Billing

## Overwhelm and Value per Second (retain by deleting)
**Use when:** customers "don't have time", "can't keep up", aren't using what they paid for; the founder keeps adding features/calls/content to reduce churn.
**Core idea:** Overwhelm is the #1 churn driver (outside price). Retention depends on customers *consuming* value, not on how much you offer. If they use one of five things, they feel they get 20% of the value; offer only that one thing and they feel they get 100%. Fix by subtraction and by making the core promise frictionless.
**How to run it:**
1. Run the keep/remove survey (twice a year): "If I removed everything but one, what would you keep?" and "If I kept everything but one, which would you least mind losing?" Chart answers; the top 1–2 are the core.
2. Pick your core 2–3 deliverables; make them excellent; cut or demote the rest.
3. Design for both extremes: a "busy path" (start-here plus weekly cadence a dabbler can keep up with from a distance) and optional depth for power users.
4. Default cadence if unsure: one big thing per week (e.g. weekly live call plus recording) and one best-of post.
5. Reduce effort and sacrifice at every step; when someone stops consuming, contact them more often, not less.
**Numbers & thresholds:** A Skool community cut content and went from 30% to 5% monthly churn — ~6x LTV (YT 2026). Gym Launch is on its 10th version, each shorter and simpler (Retention playbook).
**Worked example (from source):** A friend's $500k/month newsletter business had its lowest churn with one monthly open-ended Q&A call and one long physical newsletter. Moving to weekly calls and adding extras *increased* churn. Planet Fitness saw most members used only cardio and some weights, stripped the rest, and charged far less.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Believing problems are fixed by addition.
- Measuring "seconds of value" (more hours of content) instead of value per second.
> "Think value per second, not seconds of value." — $100M Playbook: Retention
**Ask the founder:** List everything you deliver. What % of customers use each? If you could deliver only one thing, what would it be?
**Pairs with:** Surveys & Check-ins · Activation Points
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Provide On-Going Value) · YT "How to Get Your Customers to Stay FOREVER" (2026)

## Activation Points (top priority)
**Use when:** you don't know why some customers stay and others leave; onboarding has no clear goal; deciding who to target.
**Core idea:** An activation point is a leading indicator of retention: "Customers who do X or get Y stay longer than those who don't." Find it, then (a) drive every new customer to it and (b) market to the kind of customer who reaches it. He calls it the highest-effect lever on churn.
**How to run it:**
1. Pull data on customers who stayed past a threshold (he uses 3 months by convention; any window works).
2. Rank by total spend; take the top 20% (checklist says 10–20%). That's your avatar.
3. Learn everything: demographics, psychographics, income, business size, revenue; survey if data is missing.
4. Study how they used the product and how you treated them. Common factors = activation-point candidates. Narrow to 5 and test down the list.
5. Rewrite onboarding to push everyone to the activation point fast. Rewrite ads, content and qualification to attract that avatar.
6. Retest every 6–12 months.
**Numbers & thresholds:**
- Skool hosts with at least 3 members stay far longer, so Skool helps hosts get 3 members (Retention playbook).
- Gym Launch: clients who earned back their investment in the first 30 days stayed longest; the "Fast Cash Play" pushed every client there; churn fell from 8% to 3% within 6 months (Retention playbook).
- Targeting only high-retention avatars: same CAC as a competitor, but ~$42k vs ~$5k first-year customer value and ~70x more profit (his claim, Retention playbook).
- PR agency he advised served only its best avatar and 10x'd in 24 months (anecdote).
**Common activation points (from source):** B2B service — first time they get leads; software — first login and dashboard view; physical product — first use of the consumable.
**Worked example (illustrative):** AI automation retainer. Clients who had one workflow live and saving measurable hours by week 3 renew; those still "scoping" at week 6 churn. Activation = first live workflow with a logged hours-saved number inside 21 days. Onboarding, kickoff and sales promises are rebuilt around that.
**Mistakes he calls out:** fixing retention for everyone instead of serving better customers; marketing to everyone in the niche.
**Ask the founder:** Which customers have stayed longest and paid most? What did they do in their first 30 days that churned customers didn't? When do customers first see value?
**Pairs with:** Onboarding · Incentivize Activation · `05` Pre-Call Setup (qualify for the avatar)
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #1; Author Note: Who > What)

## Onboarding (drive them to the activation point)
**Use when:** new customers stall, ask "what do I do first?", or churn in month one.
**Core idea:** Onboarding = teaching customers to hit the activation point. Any format works; some beats none. Month one is the biggest churn month, so it needs its own plan.
**How to run it:**
1. Format rules: custom > generic; personal > group; live > recorded; carrots > sticks. Expensive offer → 1-on-1; cheap offer → whatever is economical.
2. Onboarding call checklist: re-sell the value framed against *their* goals; use their name repeatedly; show exactly how to get value; explain unlockables; link them to other customers; give homework with a 7-day deadline; book the next call before hanging up.
3. Front-load the stickiest element ("move the ladder closer to the front") and pin a fast win so new members get value within ~24 hours.
4. Tell them what to cancel or change elsewhere to fund the membership, and have them prove they did it.
5. Set communication rules: how to reach you, the team and other customers; when you'll speak next. They should always know the next touchpoint.
6. Set expectations up front, including that you require a cancellation call (see Exit Interviews).
7. Optional: sell onboarding, or make them earn entry (e.g. six PT sessions before group classes).
8. Use the same onboarding logic on sales prospects — he says it raises close rate too.
**Numbers & thresholds:** A portfolio company moved from group to 1-on-1 onboarding: +25% ascensions; higher LTV let it scale ad spend, a big factor in going from $2M/month to $2M/week (Retention playbook). Day-90 milestone usually needs a real outcome or behaviour change; month 6 is usually about connection with other members (YT 2026).
**Mistakes he calls out:** no onboarding at all; leaving the time between sale and onboarding undefined (he watches time from sale to onboarding and how many customers back out).
**Ask the founder:** How many days between payment and first value? Who runs onboarding? Does every customer leave onboarding with a booked next touchpoint?
**Pairs with:** `05` Reinforce the Decision · Activation Points · Community Linking
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #2; action-step summary) · YT "How to Get Your Customers to Stay FOREVER" (2026) · YT "13 Years Of Brutally Honest Business Advice in 90 Mins" (2024)

## Incentivize Activation and Unlockables
**Use when:** customers know what to do but don't do it; churn spikes at a predictable month.
**Core idea:** Make the perfect-customer behaviour worth doing, and put rewards just past the points where people usually leave.
**How to run it:**
1. Reward activation behaviours with: unlockable courses, 1-on-1 calls or call bundles, event tickets (live/virtual), access to higher-tier calls, badges/status markers.
2. Time-based unlocks: place the first unlock just past the average lifespan, then 30 days later, then every ~3 months.
3. If most leave after month 3, make something good unlock in month 4.
4. Build one at a time; each helps on its own.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Most Skool host cancellations come from the bottom two engagement tiers (Retention playbook). Some Skool groups give free lifetime access at level 8 (a very hard level).
**Mistakes he calls out:** rewards available from day one (nothing pulls them past the churn point).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #3; Unlockables checklist)

## Usage Churn and the 5 Horsemen of Retention
**Use when:** customers are still paying but have stopped using the product; service/membership businesses wanting a simple operating routine.
**Core idea:** Usage churn (paying but not using) predicts real churn. Intercept the moment usage drops. The 5 Horsemen came from interviewing gym owners with <3% monthly churn over 6 months and finding common factors.
**How to run it (the 5 Horsemen, generalised):**
1. **Track usage/attendance.** Define the "sticks" threshold (gyms: 3+ visits/week stayed; ≤2 drifted out). Reach out the moment someone drops a level.
2. **Personal contact ~2x/week** (gyms) — praise progress, fix small problems. For higher-ticket B2B, 1-on-1 check-ins every 2–3 weeks using ACA: Acknowledge what they did, Compliment, Ask a question.
3. **Handwritten cards** at sign-up and at 3, 6, 12-month milestones (plus birthdays/holidays if you like) — made by you.
4. **Member events** on an internal calendar, regular to you and random to them (every 21, 42 or 63 days); handwritten invites. Reduces churn and produces referrals.
5. **Exit interviews** (see below).
**Numbers & thresholds:** Gyms implementing all five: month 1 churn rose ~50% (e.g. 10%→15%), month 2 fell to ~7%, month 3 to ~3% (Retention playbook). He calls the initial spike shaking the tree (printed as "shaking the three" in the playbook): people who would have cancelled anyway, plus people who had already stopped using but were still billed. Warn clients to expect it. Wealthier customers typically want *less* hand-holding (Retention playbook).
**Mistakes he calls out:** panicking at the month-one spike and stopping.
**Ask the founder:** What's the usage signal that predicts a cancel in your business? Who gets alerted when it drops, and how fast?
**Pairs with:** Activation Points · Exit Interviews
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (5 Horsemen; Results; Pro Tip: Usage Churn; Checklist #8)

## Community Linking (they stay for the people)
**Use when:** memberships, groups, cohorts, gyms, coaching — any offer where customers can meet each other; churn around month 6.
**Core idea:** Connect members to each other, not just to you. A web of many ties is stickier and more scalable than one tie to the owner.
**How to run it:**
1. Onboard in small groups on purpose (e.g. 1-on-6 instead of 1-on-1) so new people leave knowing others.
2. Manually introduce people who would benefit from knowing each other — like a host at a party: name, what they're good at, what they have in common.
3. Identify your top ~10 "true regulars" (model customers). DM them, learn their goals, spotlight them publicly, invite them to contribute, and introduce new members to them.
4. Elevate micro-celebrities by praising specific expertise in public.
5. Run group events; start a community podcast interviewing members.
6. Owner shows up daily (clear notifications, answer DMs, read complaints every morning). He says communities whose owners are active daily retain dramatically better and he has basically never seen it fail.
**Numbers & thresholds:** He cites an employee-side finding (via Leila): an employee with a real friend at work stays about 5x longer — he argues it transfers to customers (YT 2026, not his customer data).
> "It’s easy to quit a membership, it’s hard to leave a relationship." — $100M Playbook: Retention (a gym-industry saying he quotes)
**Mistakes he calls out:** new members feeling like they walked in on someone else's conversation (in-group/out-group).
**Ask the founder:** Does each new customer know at least one other customer by the end of week one? Who are your 10 regulars?
**Pairs with:** Onboarding · Correct or Fire Bad Customers
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #4) · YT "How to Get Your Customers to Stay FOREVER" (2026)

## Correct or Fire Bad Customers
**Use when:** a few customers poison a community, demand off-scope work, or drag down others' experience.
**Core idea:** Bad actors make it worse for everyone. Reward good behaviour publicly; correct bad behaviour with a reason and an example of good; remove repeat offenders.
**How to run it:** pin the best 1–2 new posts daily; praise in public; delete bad posts and explain why plus what good looks like; three strikes then out. Give posting categories including wins (testimonials), fun, discoveries (with data) and meetups.
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #5) · YT "How to Get Your Customers to Stay FOREVER" (2026)

## Annual and Upfront Billing (retention mechanics only)
**Use when:** high monthly churn; volatile monthly results; deciding billing terms. (Pricing levels and continuity-offer design → `03-money-models.md`.)
**Core idea:** Customers who pay for longer stays stay longer: they decide once instead of twelve times, are usually more committed and more qualified, and the longer look-back window lets value exceed price.
**How to run it:**
1. Add an annual option. Mandatory annual cuts both sales and churn (sometimes nets more; consider for phone/webinar sales). Optional annual suits website checkout.
2. Price it: if average stay is under 12 months, price annual *above* average lifetime revenue (avg 6 months × $1,000 → annual above $6,000). If average stay is over 12 months, use "buy 10 get 2 free" (~16–17% off).
3. Alternative — "big head, long tail": a large one-time fee for one-time value (setup, education, implementation) plus a small recurring fee for ongoing value. Sell them together in one transaction so the anchor holds.
4. Alternative — founder rates (e.g. 50% off while margins hold) to convert more and keep them longer.
5. Prefer billing upfront for longer periods over locking people into contracts; he'll trade some price for a large churn reduction.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Typically 10–20% take annual at "buy 10 get 2" when monthly remains (Retention playbook). Big head/long tail example: $6,800 upfront + $199/mo for ~30 months lifts LTV from $6,800 to $12,800 (his illustrative math; 30 × $199 ≈ $5,970, so ≈ $12,770).
**Mistakes he calls out:** selling the recurring piece as a separate second sale (loses the price anchor).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #6) · YT "How to Get Your Customers to Stay FOREVER" (2026) · YT "13 Years of No BS Business Advice in 79 Mins" (2024)

## Exit Interviews and Cancellation Calls
**Use when:** customers cancel by email/text with no conversation; you don't know why people leave.
**Core idea:** Talk to people before they leave. About half of those who get on a cancellation call can be saved; the other half gives you feedback to fix the product. Calls save more than email or text.
**How to run it:**
1. At onboarding, tell customers you require an exit interview to cancel.
2. Treat any cancellation notice as the trigger to book it.
3. On the call, let them vent. Get more outraged on their behalf than they are ("only one person can be in the angry boat") — His lines: "That’s completely ridiculous. You’re 100% correct. Will you give me the opportunity to make it right?" then "And just so we’re clear, if these things happened, you would be happy?" — the answer is your roadmap.
4. Save paths: **redo** ("Let me give it another shot and I’ll make it right.") or **upsell** (they needed more help; credit what they paid toward the higher program — "that's on me").
5. Re-sell how staying serves their goals; remind them what they'd lose (data, work done, status, custom URLs, posts, founder pricing).
6. Low-ticket/high-volume: use a cancellation video instead (why they started + what they'd lose), and a short no-pressure message, e.g. "Hey, no pressure. I would love to hear all the reasons you hate me." (Retention playbook).
7. Tag every cancel reason in a sheet (price, overwhelm, not using, missing feature, not what I expected). Every ~20 cancels, fix the top one or two. Don't react to a single cancel.
8. Ask your longest-tenured members why they stay; whatever repeats is your retention engine — do more of it and consider dropping the rest.
**Numbers & thresholds:** If half show up and half of those are saved, churn falls ~25% — about a 33% LTV increase (Retention playbook).
**Price-objection rule at cancel (YT 2026):** if *ideal* customers say it's too expensive, add a lower tier/standard plan; if non-ideal customers say it, ignore it — price is filtering correctly. Skool added a $9 tier (from $99) for hobbyists while keeping the higher tier.
**Mistakes he calls out:** ignoring "it costs too much" as "they don't get it"; knee-jerk changes from one response.
**Ask the founder:** Do you know the top reason for your last 20 cancels? Does anyone talk to a customer before the cancel goes through?
**Pairs with:** Why People Leave · Overwhelm · `03-money-models.md` (tiers/downsells)
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (5 Horsemen #5; Churn Checklist #7) · YT "How to Get Your Customers to Stay FOREVER" (2026)

## Customer Journey: Activate → Testimonial → Refer → Ascend
**Use when:** no defined post-sale path; designing what success looks like after purchase; linking retention to growth.
**Core idea:** Plan four milestones for every customer. Activation comes first; the other three can happen in any order. Each milestone can carry a reward, and they pair well with unlockables.
**How to run it:**
1. **Activate:** reach the activation point (above).
2. **Testimonial:** capture proof at the win (use the 6-point testimonial script in `05` Proof Checklist). Wins posts in a community generate these.
3. **Refer:** ask at the high points (see Referrals).
4. **Ascend:** offer the next thing they need. Customers get an itch to buy more over time — from you or someone else. Someone who just bought again is the least likely to churn.
5. Map each milestone to an owner, a trigger and a reward.
**Mistakes he calls out:** forgetting to sell existing customers again.
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Retention (Churn Checklist #9; checklist summary)

## Referrals From Customers
**Use when:** founder wants cheaper customers; CAC rising; retention improving but growth flat.
**Core idea:** Happy customers are a lead source. Ask directly, ask often, and make it easy. Referral growth beating churn is what makes a business compound.
**How to run it:**
1. Ask in a way that needs a name, not a yes/no: "Who do you know who's as [awesome/driven] as you who'd benefit from this?"
2. Ask at purchase (end of the sales call or right after paying), at early wins, and at 3/6/12-month milestone cards.
3. Events and member get-togethers double as referral moments (bring a friend).
4. If they say "no one" and rapport is strong, a playful challenge works for him ("...so you want your friends to suffer?"), then address any real doubt.
5. Track referrals per month against cancels per month.
**Numbers & thresholds:** A new rep who simply asked "who else would you like to bring?" at the end of every call outperformed the next rep by ~50%; only 1 in 3–4 people referred, but he says referrals closed at 80–90% (anecdote, YT 2024). If one in three customers refers someone who buys, CAC per customer falls to roughly a third, by his math. His Leads book blurb credits referral methods with ~30% of his sales (via research notes).
**Mistakes he calls out:** waiting until "they're happy enough" rather than asking at purchase and at wins.
**Pairs with:** Customer Journey · `05` Reinforce the Decision · `04-leads-and-nurture.md` (Lead Getters)
**Sources:** YT "Sales Was Hard Until I Understood These 9 Concepts" (2024) · $100M Playbook: Retention (handwritten cards; events; compounding) · research notes on $100M Leads (referrals)

## Ascension, Re-engagement and Expansion (retention side)
**Use when:** customers finish a program and leave; one-and-done buyers; former customers going cold.
**Core idea:** Lifetime value grows when customers buy again. On the retention side: keep them buying the same thing longer (lower churn), re-engage past customers on a schedule, and move people up instead of letting them leave. Design of upsell/continuity/cross-sell offers lives in `03-money-models.md`.
**How to run it:**
1. **Ascend instead of renew at a lower tier** when programs end; consider an incentive for immediate renewal or ascension (YT 2026 discussion).
2. **Use the save-by-upsell path** in cancellation calls when the real issue is they needed more help.
3. **Reactivation:** run a quarterly promotion to past customers and leads; the rest of the time just give value. Example ideas from source: a quarterly "best lawn" contest, a quarterly challenge, a new module "launch".
4. **Make it the customer's next problem.** His favourite upsell: more of, or more help with, what they just bought — faster, less risk, less effort — for more money. Offering something far from the core product flops.
5. **Ask customers** before building expansion: in his example, 85% of surveyed customers chose the offer closest to what they already bought vs 15% for the founders' passion product.
**Numbers & thresholds:** That upsell 2.2x LTV per customer, allowed 5x ad spend profitably and took revenue from a few million per month to a few million per week (LTV playbook, his anecdote). A back-end product priced right only needs ~1 in 5 customers to buy it to double revenue (LTV playbook, his estimate).
**Ask the founder:** What do customers buy next — from anyone — after finishing with you? What % of past customers have you contacted in the last 90 days?
**Pairs with:** `03-money-models.md` (Crazy 8, upsell/continuity design) · Customer Journey
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value (Increasing LTV story; #3 Increase # of Purchases) · YT "If I Wanted To Scale A Service Business In 2026, Here's What I'd Do" (2026)

## LTGP and CAC Tolerance (how retention funds acquisition)
**Use when:** "how much can I spend to get a customer?"; ads "too expensive"; comparing your economics to a competitor; any LTV question.
**Core idea:** LTV in Hormozi's usage is *gross profit* over the customer's life (LTGP), not revenue. Attention gets more expensive over time; the business that makes a customer worth the most can outbid everyone for them. Retention raises LTGP, which raises how much CAC you can tolerate.
**How to run it:**
1. **Gross profit per unit:** revenue minus cost to deliver (not overheads). Service example from source: 10 clients per $6k/mo rep at $3k each → $30k revenue, $24k gross profit → 80% margin → $2,400/client/month.
2. **Lifetime transactions:** export customer data and average purchases per customer; or for recurring, use churn.
3. **LTGP:** transactional = gross profit × average transactions; recurring = monthly gross profit ÷ monthly churn ($2,400 ÷ 5% = $48,000).
4. **CAC:** total acquisition cost (ads, sales salaries, commissions) ÷ new customers in the period.
5. **Compare LTGP:CAC** to the thresholds below. If too low: raise LTGP (retention here; monetization in `03`) before cutting CAC.
6. **Retention budget:** reinvest up to ~1/5 of CAC per customer in keeping them.
**Numbers & thresholds:** LTGP:CAC of at least 3:1 is the commonly cited floor from *$100M Leads*. In a Jan 2026 podcast (Ep 990, via secondary summary) he refines it: 3:1 suits businesses with no humans in attraction, conversion or delivery (~5% of businesses, e.g. SaaS); human-involved businesses need ~6:1 to 9:1 because of training, turnover and inconsistency. Treat the second as current guidance for service businesses.
**Worked example (illustrative):** Agency at $3k/mo, 70% margin ($2,100 GP), 8% churn → LTGP ≈ $26,250. CAC $6,000 → 4.4:1 — fine for SaaS, thin for a human-delivered service. Cutting churn to 4% → LTGP ≈ $52,500 → 8.75:1, which also means it could afford ~2x the CAC to outbid competitors.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Using revenue LTV instead of gross profit.
- Treating LTV as fixed — it rises as a business ages because old customers buy more; estimates are always estimates.
> "he who can make his customer more valuable than his competition, wins." — $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value
**Source arithmetic flags:** the LTV playbook's product example says $80 × 4 = $360 (it's $320). The Retention playbook says 9% → 3% churn is a 3.3x LTV increase (it's 3x; 10% → 3% is the 3.3x case).
**Ask the founder:** What's your gross margin after delivery labour? Monthly churn? Fully-loaded CAC? Are humans involved in selling or delivery?

## Procedure: Diagnose My Churn
**Use when:** "my churn is high", "clients keep leaving", "revenue flat despite sales", "how do I keep customers longer?"
**Step 1 — Get the real numbers (ask for them):**
- Customers on the 1st of each of the last 3–6 months, and how many of each starting group left → monthly churn (exclude new sign-ups).
- Churn by tenure cohort: month 1, months 2–3, months 4–6, 6+.
- Joins vs cancels per month → label growing / flat / declining.
- Gross profit per customer per month, CAC → LTGP and LTGP:CAC.
- Billing frequency and contract terms.
**Step 2 — Benchmark:** compare with the Churn Math thresholds (online/consumer services: ~20% average, <10% strong, 30%+ weak; under 3% "great money"). If retention <70% monthly, retention is the #1 priority.
**Step 3 — Locate the leak by cohort:**

| Where churn concentrates | Most likely cause | First levers |
|---|---|---|
| Month 1 (or refunds/chargebacks in days) | Wrong promise, wrong customer, no reinforcement, slow first value | `05` Reinforce · Onboarding with 24-hour fast win · qualify for the avatar · align sales promises |
| Months 2–3 (before day 90) | No outcome or behaviour change yet; overwhelm | Activation point + Fast-Cash-style first-30-day result · cut to core 2–3 deliverables · usage alerts |
| Months 4–6 | Value fading; no connections | Unlockables past churn points · community linking · events |
| Steady churn at all tenures | Price-to-value gap; billing too frequent | Look-back window → annual/upfront billing · surveys · exit interviews |
| Spikes after billing events / renewals | Renewal moment = decision moment | Ascend-at-renewal offer · re-sell value before the bill |

**Step 4 — Find why (qualitative):**
1. Tag the last 20+ cancel reasons (price / overwhelm / not using / missing feature / not what I expected).
2. Interview the longest-tenured, highest-spend customers: "What made you stay? What's the #1 value?"
3. Run the keep/remove survey.
4. Check usage churn: who is paying but inactive right now?
**Step 5 — Prescribe (pick the top 1–3 only, in this order unless data says otherwise):**
1. Activation point → onboarding redesign.
2. Delete/simplify to the core (if overwhelm or low usage).
3. Exit interviews required + tagging loop.
4. Usage-drop outreach (ACA check-ins) and handwritten milestone cards.
5. Community linking / regulars / events.
6. Annual or upfront billing option.
7. Unlockables just past the main churn month.
8. Tiering only if *ideal* customers cite price.
9. Customer journey with testimonial, referral and ascension asks.
**Step 6 — Set expectations and measure:** warn that churn may rise in the first month as inactive payers cancel ("shaking the tree"). Re-measure monthly by cohort for 3 months. Translate results into LTV and LTGP:CAC so the founder sees the dollar effect. Add one lever at a time; he says retention is 100 small things done consistently.
**Output format for the founder:** (a) churn number and cohort curve, (b) LTGP and LTGP:CAC now vs at target churn, (c) top 3 causes with evidence, (d) 3 actions with owner, trigger and metric, (e) 90-day re-check date.
