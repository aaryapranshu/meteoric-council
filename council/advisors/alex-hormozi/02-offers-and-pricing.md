# 02 — Offers & Pricing

**When to open this file:** the founder says the offer isn't converting, leads say "too expensive", close rate is weird (very high or very low), they want to raise prices, name a product, add a guarantee/bonus/deadline, pick a niche, or needs cash fast from existing customers.
**Order of diagnosis Hormozi uses:** market → offer → price → persuasion. Don't fix copy or sales scripts until market and offer are right.
**Not here:** how to sequence multiple offers, 30-day cash, CAC/LTGP ratios, upsells/downsells/continuity → `03-money-models.md`.
**End of file:** "Offer Teardown Checklist" — run it on any offer the user pastes.

## Market Selection ("Starving Crowd") & Niching
**Use when:** "Who should I sell to?", "my offer is good but nobody buys", founder serves "everyone", or is choosing between niches.
**Core idea:** The market matters more than the offer, and the offer matters more than the persuasion. You channel demand that already exists. You don't create it. A great offer in a dead market still loses.
**How to run it:**
1. Score each candidate market 1–5 on the four criteria: **massive pain**, **purchasing power**, **easy to target** (you can find and reach them in a list, group, platform or event), **growing**.
2. Kill any market that scores low on pain or purchasing power. No amount of copy fixes those two.
3. Check it sits in or near an evergreen bucket: **health, wealth, relationships**. B2B almost always maps to wealth.
4. Niche down until the offer can name the person. Stacking niche qualifiers (role + industry + stage + situation) lets you charge more, because you look like the specialist.
5. Commit. Run many offer iterations inside one market before you switch markets.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Four criteria and three evergreen markets per *$100M Offers* (p.35–37, via summaries). The free Scaling Course's Stage 4 ("Prioritize", 5–9 employees) constraint is "trying to be everything to everyone". The fix is to niche down to people like your best customers.
**Worked example (illustrative):** "Accounting for small businesses" scores low on targeting and differentiation. "Fractional CFO for med spas and anti-aging clinics" is growing, has money, is easy to list, and has pain about margin and staffing. Hormozi walks through almost this exact example in a 2024 video.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Picking a market because you love it rather than because it pays.
- Switching markets after a few failed offers. The offer failed, not the market. "If your offer doesn't work, it doesn't mean you suck. It means your offer sucks." ($100M Offers p.40, via summary)
- Serving 2–3 avatars with one price and one message. See *Rules of Pricing*: different avatars need different prices.
**Ask the founder:** Who are your 3 best customers? What do they have in common? Can you buy or build a list of 1,000 more like them this week? Do they already spend money trying to solve this problem?

## The Value Equation
**Use when:** "How do I make my offer more valuable?", "they say it's too expensive", "they don't believe it'll work", comparing your offer with a competitor's, or rewriting ad and sales copy.
**Core idea:** **Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Perceived Time Delay × Perceived Effort & Sacrifice).** Two levers go up and two go down. As the bottom of the fraction approaches zero, value grows without limit. Every lever is *perceived*, so proof and framing count as much as the actual delivery.
**How to run it:**
1. **Dream outcome:** write the result in the buyer's words, including the status gain ("people walk into my home and are impressed", not "a table"). Sell the outcome, not the vehicle.
2. **Likelihood:** list every reason they doubt it will work for *them*. Counter each one with proof that looks like them (same industry and size), specific numbers, recent results, a guarantee, or a mechanism.
3. **Time delay:** find the first fast win you can deliver inside 24–72 hours. Shrink time-to-first-result before you shrink time-to-full-result.
4. **Effort & sacrifice:** list every hidden cost (driving, learning, changing habits, internal politics, migration). Remove or absorb them: DFY instead of DIY, templates, integrations, "we do the setup".
5. Turn each fix into an offer component or a line of copy. In ads, the same four levers become your callouts.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Pricing up can itself raise *perceived likelihood*. In a 2026 Scale or Fail episode, a duct-cleaning company raised price 23% and its close rate went *up*. Hormozi's explanation: a higher price raised buyers' conviction that the company could deliver. A price that is too low makes you look like a "duct-tape operation".
**Worked example (from source):** A relaxation pill scores high on likelihood, low on delay, zero on effort. Meditation scores worse on all three, so to sell it you must prove the method, get people relaxed in 5 minutes, and get them proficient in weeks.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Only pumping the dream outcome (bigger promises) while ignoring delay and effort. Speed and ease often win: "fast beats free" (book).
- Claims without proof. The same promise from someone with 11,000 reviews beats a bigger promise from someone with one review (YT, 2025).
- Confusing logical and psychological fixes. Sometimes the cheapest fix is perception (the classic "mirrors by the elevator").
**Ask the founder:** What's the #1 reason qualified people *don't* buy? How long until a new customer sees a first result? What do they have to do or give up to get it? What proof do you have from customers who look like the prospect?

## Finding Hidden Costs: "What Would It Take?" + the Magic Question
**Use when:** you're building an offer from scratch, prospects say no and you don't know why, or you're at Stage 0–1 (no paid customers yet).
**Core idea:** People don't buy because of hidden, non-monetary costs. Ask them, with the price already set, what would make it a yes. Then decide which of those things you'd do *at a price*.
**How to run it:**
1. After a no, ask: "What would it take?" This assumes the purchase and works backwards into features.
2. Magic question, with price baked in: "If we worked together for $[X] over [time] to get [goal], what would you need to see to be excited to move forward?"
3. List every answer as a hidden cost: travel, time, skills, risk, internal approval.
4. For each answer, ask yourself "would I do that for [much higher price]?" Most "I'd never do that" decisions were made without a price attached.
5. Build the components you'd do into the offer, then price it at a premium.
**Worked example (from source):** A trainer whose clients won't come in. "Would you pick them up for $1,000 a session?" Probably yes. The constraint was the price, not the willingness.
**Mistakes he calls out:** Refusing to do "unscalable" things before you have one customer. Get paid first, then make it scalable.
**Ask the founder:** What did the last 5 people who said no actually say? What would you do if they paid 3x?
**Pairs with:** Value Equation · Grand Slam Offer Build · Fast Cash Play (unscalable value)
**Sources:** Acquisition.com Scaling Course, Stage 1 "Monetize"

## Grand Slam Offer Build (Problems → Solutions → Delivery → Trim & Stack)
**Use when:** "Build me an offer", "my offer is commoditised", "they compare me to cheaper competitors", or launching a new product or service.
**Core idea:** A Grand Slam Offer bundles solutions to *every* obstacle between the buyer and their dream outcome, so it can't be compared with anything else. That puts you in a "category of one": the buyer weighs your offer against doing nothing, not against a competitor. The book's definition also includes a payment structure that lets you get paid to acquire customers (→ file 03).
**How to run it:**
1. **Dream outcome.** One sentence, buyer's words, specific.
2. **List every problem.** Walk through each step the buyer takes before, during and after buying. For each step, write the obstacle, then run it through the four value levers: "it won't work for me", "it'll take too long", "it's too hard", "I'll have to give up X". Aim for 20+ problems. One unsolved problem can be the single reason someone doesn't buy.
3. **Solutions list.** Turn each problem into a "how to…" solution and give it a name.
4. **Delivery vehicles (the "delivery cube").** For each solution, list the ways you could deliver it:
   - **Personal attention:** 1:1 / small group / one-to-many
   - **Effort required from them:** DIY / DWY (done with you) / DFY (done for you)
   - **Environment:** live / recorded · in person / remote
   - **Format:** call, video, doc, template, software, checklist
   - **Speed and access:** response time, days and hours of availability
5. **Trim & stack.** Score each delivery option on value to the customer and cost to you.
   - Keep high-value / low-cost items.
   - Cut low-value / high-cost items.
   - Keep high-value / high-cost items only if the price supports them (premium tiers and Fast Cash Plays live here).
   - Bundle what's left into one named offer.
6. **Add enhancers** (bonuses, guarantee, scarcity, urgency, name). See the cards below.
7. **Price against value, not cost.** See *Pricing Philosophy*.
**Numbers & thresholds:** None hard. As a rule of thumb from the bonuses section, the stacked bonus value should exceed the core offer's value.
**Worked example (illustrative, B2B AI consultancy):** Dream outcome: "Ops team handles 2x volume without new hires in 90 days." Problems include "our data is a mess", "staff won't adopt it", "IT will block it", "we tried tools before and they failed", "we don't know what to automate first". Solutions become: a data-cleanup sprint (DFY), an adoption playbook plus team training (DWY), a security pack for IT (template), a past-failure audit, and a "first 3 automations" prioritisation workshop. Trim the custom dashboard (high cost, low value) and keep the templates and recorded training (low cost, high value).
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Selling the vehicle ("gym membership", "AI agent") instead of the outcome ("lose 20 lb", "cut response time to 5 min").
- Trimming before you've listed everything. List first, cut second.
- Copying competitors' packaging. Your shape becomes their shape and price becomes the only difference.
- Lowering price to close instead of adding value (see Bonuses).
**Ask the founder:** What's the outcome in the buyer's words? What are the 10 most common reasons a good-fit prospect says no? Which parts of delivery cost you the most, and do customers actually value them? What could you templatise once and give to everyone?
**Pairs with:** Value Equation · Bonuses · Guarantees · Naming · `03-money-models.md`
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, "Offer Creation Pt 1 & 2", via summaries) · free $100M Offers Course

## Bonuses
**Use when:** a prospect is on the fence, you're tempted to discount, the offer feels thin, or you're running a launch or Fast Cash campaign.
**Core idea:** Bonuses raise demand without lowering price. Split the offer into named parts and stack them so perceived value far exceeds the price.
**How to run it:**
1. Each bonus solves one specific obstacle or objection. Name it after the problem it solves.
2. Prefer one-time-build assets that cost little to deliver: checklists, scripts, templates, swipe files, recorded workshops, SOPs, vetted vendor lists.
3. Assign each bonus a value and show it.
4. Optionally add scarcity or urgency to the bonuses themselves ("first 10 get…", "bonus expires Friday").
5. You can partner with adjacent businesses to supply bonuses: their product, your customer.
6. In a live sales sequence, reveal bonuses one at a time. Each one adds new information that widens the gap between price and value (see Fast Cash Play).
**Numbers & thresholds:** Stacked bonus value should eclipse the core offer's value (book, via summary).
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Discounting to close. Adding bonuses is far better than cutting price, because discounts teach buyers your price is negotiable.
- Throwing in random extras that don't match an objection.
**Ask the founder:** What are the top 3 objections you hear? What have you already built (docs, trainings, templates) that you could package as a bonus at near-zero marginal cost?
**Pairs with:** Grand Slam Offer Build · Fast Cash Play · Scarcity & Urgency
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, via summaries) · $100M Playbook: Fast Cash

## Guarantees (and the Four Guarantee Types)
**Use when:** "They're worried it won't work", "how do I reduce risk for the buyer", high-ticket or B2B deals stall, or the founder asks what guarantee to offer.
**Core idea:** The biggest objection to any purchase is risk. A guarantee reverses it, which raises perceived likelihood in the Value Equation. Match the guarantee type to your delivery cost and how much control the buyer has over the result.
**How to run it:**
1. Pick a type:

| Type | What it is | Best for | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unconditional** | Pay now, refund if unhappy, no questions asked. The strongest type. | Low COGS, strong product, B2C | Attracts some people who buy *because* of it. Refund cost must be survivable. |
| **Conditional** | "Do X, and if you don't get Y in Z time, you get [refund / more service / costs covered]." | High-ticket, B2B, outcome depends on client effort | Make the conditions the actions that actually produce the result, so the guarantee *drives* compliance. |
| **Anti-guarantee** | "All sales final", with a creative reason (e.g., you get proprietary assets or access instantly). | High delivery cost, consumable IP, custom work | Needs a credible reason or it reads as a red flag. |
| **Implied (performance)** | Revenue share, profit share, pay-per-result, ratchets: "if I don't perform, I don't get paid." | B2B with measurable outcomes | You carry the risk and must be able to measure and collect. |

2. Conditional variants to choose from: keep working with them until they get the result; refund ancillary costs; pay them for their time ("wage payment"); release them from the contract; delay the second payment until a milestone; cover their costs until the first outcome.
3. **Stack** guarantees where it helps (e.g., 30-day unconditional + 90-day conditional outcome guarantee).
4. **Name it** ("Show-Up-or-Shut-Up Guarantee"). A named guarantee feels more real.
5. Put it in writing and in the sales script.
**Numbers & thresholds:** None hard. Book advice when starting out: begin with service-based guarantees ("we keep working until…") or performance partnerships.
**Worked examples:**
- (2026 Scale or Fail) A parent-coaching program whose buyers were mostly mothers deciding without their spouse got a 3-day "no sweat" guarantee. It handles the spouse objection and is often legally required anyway.
- (2026 Scale or Fail) An events business added a price-lock guarantee plus multi-year and prepaid options. Average contract went from about $44k to about $200k.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Big unconditional guarantees on high-cost fulfilment.
- Vague guarantees ("satisfaction guaranteed") that buyers ignore.
- Forgetting the guarantee is a sales tool: if it isn't in the pitch, it does nothing.
- A third-party wiki lists a "zero guarantee" type from one video. It's unverified, so don't present it as canon.
**Ask the founder:** What does a refund cost you (COGS, time, opportunity)? How much of the result depends on the client's effort? Can you measure the outcome? What's the risk the buyer actually fears: money, time, embarrassment in front of the boss?
**Pairs with:** Value Equation · Grand Slam Offer · Instant Profit Pricing Plays (#10)
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, via summaries) · $100M Playbook: Pricing · YT "If You Hate Selling, Please Watch This | Scale or Fail" (2026) · YT "8 Entrepreneurs Compete For $100,000" (2026)

## Scarcity (limited supply)
**Use when:** you have real capacity limits, you're launching, or you want buyers to act now rather than "later".
**Core idea:** Restrict supply to raise demand and justify a higher price. Fear of loss is stronger than desire for gain (book).
**How to run it:**
1. Choose a form: total cap ("only 10 spots ever"), per-period cap ("4 new clients per month"), cohort cap, or limited bonuses ("first 5 get…").
2. Base it on real constraints: team capacity, your personal time, inventory.
3. Publicly report sell-through ("6 of 10 gone", "2 left", "sold out").
4. Waitlist anyone who tried to buy after you sold out, and get permission to charge them if a spot opens.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Fast Cash cap: 5–10% of your customer base, low enough that you *will* sell out (Fast Cash playbook).
**Mistakes he calls out:** Fake scarcity you can't defend. Never invent a "someone backed out" spot. Only send that message if it's true.
**Ask the founder:** What's your real capacity per month? What would you do if it sold out in a day?
**Pairs with:** Urgency · Fast Cash Play
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, via summaries) · $100M Playbook: Fast Cash

## Urgency (limited time)
**Use when:** leads sit on the fence, pipeline is full of "maybe later", or you're planning promotions.
**Core idea:** Urgency lowers the threshold for action by making waiting cost something.
**How to run it:**
1. Pick a mechanism:
   - **Rolling cohorts:** "Next group starts the 1st."
   - **Rolling seasonal urgency:** the same promo renamed by season ("New Year Kickoff ends Jan 31").
   - **Pricing deadline:** "Price goes up Monday."
   - **Bonus deadline:** "Bonus expires Friday."
   - **Exploding opportunity:** the offer expires shortly after it's made.
2. Put the deadline in every message. Count down.
3. Honour it. If deadlines are never enforced, urgency stops working.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Fast Cash campaigns run 7 days or less. Any longer loses urgency, any shorter misses sales (Fast Cash playbook).
**Mistakes he calls out:** "Ends soon" with no date. Extending deadlines.
**Ask the founder:** What real event can anchor the deadline (cohort start, price change, season, capacity)?
**Pairs with:** Scarcity · Fast Cash Play · Price Raise Playbook (pair raises with launches)
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, via summaries) · $100M Playbook: Fast Cash

## Naming the Offer (M-A-G-I-C)
**Use when:** "What should I call this?", the offer has a generic name ("Coaching Package", "Growth Plan"), or ad response is fatiguing and needs a refresh.
**Core idea:** A good name re-stimulates demand and tells the right buyer "this is for you".
**How to run it:** Use 3–5 of the five components:
- **M — Magnetic reason why:** why this exists or why now ("Free", "Launch", "Back-to-School").
- **A — Announce the avatar:** who it's for ("for Med Spas", "for Busy Dads").
- **G — Give them a goal:** the outcome ("2x Bookings", "Lose 20 lb").
- **I — Indicate a time interval:** "in 30 Days", "6-Week".
- **C — Complete with a container word:** Blueprint, Challenge, System, Sprint, Accelerator, Intensive.
Then test 2–3 names in ads or outreach and keep the winner. Rename periodically when response drops.
**Worked example (illustrative):** "AI Ops Sprint" → "The 30-Day Zero-New-Hires Ops Sprint for Logistics Founders" (M: zero new hires, A: logistics founders, G: implied capacity, I: 30-day, C: Sprint).
**Mistakes he calls out:** Clever names that don't say who it's for or what it gets them. Never refreshing a tired name. For tiers, name each one after an aspirational identity the buyer wants (Pricing playbook rule).
**Ask the founder:** Who exactly is this for? What do they get, and by when?
**Pairs with:** Grand Slam Offer · Rules of Pricing (tier names)
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, via summaries) · $100M Playbook: Pricing

## Commoditised vs Differentiated (Category of One)
**Use when:** "Competitors are cheaper", "clients shop us on price", or the founder wants to lower prices to compete.
**Core idea:** If buyers can line up your offer against others feature by feature, the cheapest one wins. Change the offer so the comparison disappears. Use more components, a different delivery vehicle, a guarantee and a specific avatar, so the buyer is choosing between your offer and nothing.
**How to run it:**
1. List how a buyer would compare you with the top 3 alternatives today.
2. For each comparable dimension, add or change something the alternatives can't easily match: a speed promise, a DFY component, a guarantee, a niche-specific asset.
3. Reposition the dream outcome around status or ROI, not the commodity function.
4. Re-price against value (see the next card).
**Worked example (from source):** Tables. A $50–100 plastic table and a $20,000 hand-carved table both hold your dinner. Reposition the dream outcome as "guests are impressed when they walk in", and add hand delivery and perks. His illustration takes net profit per table from $20 to $620, roughly 31x. He notes that within an industry CAC is roughly similar for competent marketers, so the winners are the ones who make more per customer.
**Mistakes he calls out:** Competing on price. He quotes Dan Kennedy: being the second-lowest price in a market has no strategic advantage, but being the highest does. Assuming B2B is price-sensitive.
**Ask the founder:** If a buyer put you next to your top competitor, what would they compare? What could you add that they couldn't copy in 30 days?
**Pairs with:** Grand Slam Offer · Pricing Philosophy
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, via summaries) · YT "How to Make Money So Fast It Feels ILLEGAL" (2024) · $100M Playbook: Price Raise

## Pricing Philosophy: Premium Pricing & the Virtuous Cycle of Price
**Use when:** "Should I charge more?", "I'm afraid to raise prices", "I'm cheap so more people can afford it", the founder is broke or barely profitable, or clients are demanding and get poor results.
**Core idea:** Higher prices make better businesses and better customers. **Virtuous cycle:** raise price → clients' emotional investment rises → perceived value rises → results improve → clients get *less* demanding → you have more money per client to deliver → better service → stronger sales conviction. Lowering price runs the same cycle in reverse (the vicious cycle): the cheapest clients ask for the most.
> "Profit is unnatural. You must force it into existence." — $100M Playbook: Pricing

**How to run it:**
1. Decide to be priced at the top of the market and use the extra gross profit to deliver far more value. Premium price funds a superior product, which leaves the customer with a surplus and leaves you with profit.
2. Check the signals that price is too low (see Rules of Pricing).
3. Test higher prices on new customers first (see Price Raise Playbook).
4. B2B: sell on ROI. Price as a fraction of the value created.
**Numbers & thresholds:**
- Book: about 99% of businesses need to *raise* prices to grow, not lower them (via summary).
- Service businesses: target **≥80% gross margin**. Each step from 80→90→95% roughly doubles productivity again (YT 2021, his rule of thumb).
- "Until customers tell you your prices are too high, they're probably too low." (X, Primary)
- "Businesses aren't price sensitive, they're ROI sensitive." (X, Primary)
- On X he says that if you're on your last dollar and can't get customers, raise prices. Don't lower them.
**Worked examples (from sources):**
- Trey's gym, priced at $49/month, had the worst customers and was losing money. He tripled prices on all members, lost some, and went from losing $2k/month to making $4k/month within about a week (Price Raise playbook).
- Hormozi's own gym went from $99 to $299 and lost 30% of customers: 200 × $99 became 140 × $299. Revenue more than doubled and delivery costs fell 30% (Pricing playbook).
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- Pricing by looking at what everyone else charges. His point: most of those competitors are broke, so copying them copies their economics.
- Believing cheap price = more impact. It usually means worse results and a business that dies.
- Raising price without raising value for big jumps: the bigger the increase, the bigger the value increase should be.
- Not believing in your price. If you don't, make the product better until you do.
**Ask the founder:** What's your gross margin per client? What's your close rate on qualified calls? What would you do for clients if you had 2x the money per client?
**Pairs with:** Rules of Pricing · Price Raise Playbook · `03-money-models.md` (LTGP:CAC)
**Sources:** $100M Offers (book, ch. "Charge Its Worth", via summaries) · $100M Playbook: Price Raise · $100M Playbook: Pricing · YT "If You Don't Understand Margin, You Don't Understand Business" (2021) · Hormozi on X

## Why Price Beats Volume (the Genie Math)
**Use when:** the founder wants more leads or customers but is under-priced, or asks "what's the highest-leverage thing to fix?"
**Core idea:** Doubling customers, doubling purchase frequency or doubling price each doubles revenue, but doubling price hits *profit* hardest, because costs don't double with it.
**Numbers & thresholds (from the Pricing playbook's model business: $10k/month, 20% net, 30 new clients/month, 33% churn, $100/month):**

| Lever doubled | Profit result |
|---|---|
| 2x new customers (CAC halves) | ~3.5x profit |
| 2x purchases / retention | ~3.5x profit |
| **2x price** | **~6x profit** (net margin 20% → 60%) |

- Profitwell data quoted in the playbook (512 companies): a 1% improvement in **monetisation** lifts profit about **12.7%**, retention about **6.71%**, acquisition about **3.32%**. Companies with continual price optimisation had much better LTV/CAC (11.09 vs 1.68 with no pricing function, in a 2015 survey of 96 SaaS companies with >$5M ARR).
- A 10% price increase at 10% net margin doubles profit. A 20% increase at 10% margin triples it (LTV playbook).
- A 4% processing fee on a $1M restaurant at 9% net takes profit from $90k to $130k (+44%) (Pricing playbook).
**Note:** the playbook's tables contradict themselves in places (Option #1 shows both $4k and $7k profit). Quote the multiples, not the line items.
**Ask the founder:** What are your net margins? When did you last change price?
**Pairs with:** Pricing Philosophy · Instant Profit Pricing Plays
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Pricing · $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value

## The Three Pricing Models & Finding the Value-Driven Price
**Use when:** "How do I set my price?", "what should I charge?", or comparing price points.
**Core idea:** There are three models. **Cost-plus** (cost plus a margin) ignores what buyers would pay. **Competitor-based** (the market average) copies someone else's economics. **Value-based** (what the customer will pay for the outcome) is the target. The best price is the one that makes the most money over the customer's lifetime, not the one that gets the most customers or the biggest first purchase.
**How to run it:**
1. Track for each price point: traffic or leads, conversion rate, sales, churn (or repeat rate), LTGP, total return.
2. Total return = sales × LTGP. Pick the price with the highest total return.
3. Test the gaps between winning points.
4. Re-test regularly. He tests prices quarterly.
**Numbers & thresholds (playbook example):**

| Price | Conv. | Sales /100 clicks | Monthly churn | LTV | Total return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10 | 5% | 5 | 10% | $100 | $500 |
| $20 | 4% | 4 | 10% | $200 | **$800 (+60%)** |
| $100 | 2% | 2 | 33% | $300 | $600 (+20%) |

Doubling price cut conversion only 20%, so it wins. The 10x price still beats baseline but loses to 2x. Next step: test $39–59.
- Break-even rule: compute the conversion rate at which the new price earns the same gross profit as the old one. If actual conversion stays above it, the new price wins. Keep stepping up until conversion × LTGP drops.
**Worked example (2026 Scale or Fail):** A company closing over 80% got a proposed 10% raise. Break-even close rate was about 65%: as long as they close above 65%, they make more. In that business, a 10% raise was about +25% net profit.
**Mistakes he calls out:** Optimising for the most customers. Reverting the price after the first "no". Mixing up revenue LTV and gross-profit LTV (always use gross profit).
**Ask the founder:** What's your current conversion at each step, and your churn or repeat rate? What's your gross margin?

## Rules of Pricing (Hormozi's diagnostics)
**Use when:** a quick check on whether price is wrong, or questions about billing frequency, tiers, or bundling one-time and ongoing value.
**Core idea:** A fixed set of rules he applies before any tactic.

| Rule | What it means / threshold |
|---|---|
| **High close rate = prices too low** | Playbook: over 50% closes consistently means there's room. In 2026 videos he flags 80%+ ("there's room") and treats 90% as "aggressively raise". |
| **Full capacity = prices too low** | If you're sold out, or have fixed capacity and keep filling it, raise. |
| **Keep raising until extra money from new sales no longer offsets lost sales** | Expect to hear many more nos. Going from a 50% to a 30% close rate while doubling price makes more money. |
| **Raising price makes money two ways** | More gross profit per customer, plus lower delivery cost because you serve fewer customers. |
| **Price for lifetime, not first purchase** | Use the price people *keep paying*, not the highest one they'll pay once. |
| **Too high signals** | Sales stop, or people complain about value for cost (he measures with NPS). |
| **Different avatars → different prices** | One avatar often has 5–10x the willingness to pay of another. Use tiers, make clear which tier is for whom, and name tiers aspirationally. This is an advanced move that needs operational chops. |
| **Bill less frequently** | More billing events mean more chances to cancel. Bill on the longest cycle you can. |
| **Lookback window of value** | Buyers judge value within the last billing cycle, not the whole relationship (his stated theory). Longer cycles mean less churn and more qualified buyers. |
| **Display smallest, bill largest** | "$5/day", billed $1,825/year. |
| **Separate one-time vs ongoing value** | Charge a one-time fee for information or setup and a smaller recurring fee for ongoing support. Bundling them underprices the first and overprices the second, which drives churn. |
| **Customer surplus** | Value minus price drives word of mouth and repeat buying. Charge premium *and* over-deliver. |
| **Believe in your price** | If you don't, improve the product until you do. |

**Ask the founder:** Close rate? Capacity utilisation? Billing frequency? Do you sell setup and ongoing work as one price?
**Pairs with:** Instant Profit Pricing Plays · Price Raise Playbook
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Pricing · YT "Building a $2,500,000 Business for a Stranger…" (2026) · YT "8 Entrepreneurs Compete For $100,000" (2026)

## Instant Profit Pricing Plays (the 10 low-risk price levers)
**Use when:** "How do I make more profit without more customers?", the founder is scared of a big price raise, or you want quick wins this month.
**Core idea:** Ten small moves designed to barely affect conversion. Each drops almost entirely to profit. He says you only need one or two. Pick the biggest impact for the least work and risk.

| # | Play | How | Est. lift (playbook) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Monthly → 28-day billing** | Bill every 4 weeks: 13 cycles per year, not 12. Display the weekly price, bill every 4 weeks. He advises against weekly or bi-weekly (too many pause hassles). Change contracts for new customers now and set a date for existing ones. | 8.3% |
| 2 | **Processing fee + second payment method** | After price agreement: "How did you want to pay?" → "It's just a 3.99% card processing fee." If they balk, waive it in exchange for a backup payment method. The backup card cuts involuntary churn, which runs 1.2–1.7%/month and can be 24–34% of total churn at 5% churn. In his example LTV rises up to +51%. Re-run failed charges the next day on the backup. | 3–4% (more via churn) |
| 3 | **Sales tax** | If you're legally liable, add it as a separate line on the invoice with the statute cited, matter-of-fact. If they complain, get indignant alongside them. You can't charge tax you don't owe. Check with lawyers about incorporating somewhere else. | 0–10% |
| 4 | **Annual price increase in contracts** | Write a 5–15% annual increase (or CPI) into new contracts. Raise it during paperwork, not the pitch. Script: reinvesting to keep quality as costs rise. Few balk under 15%. | 3–10% |
| 5 | **Annual / longer billing options** | Profitwell data: monthly churn 10.7% on monthly billing, 5% on quarterly, 2% on annual, so annual LTV is ~5.35x. Anchor with the full annual price first, then offer a prepay discount (~16–17%). If they decline, offer quarterly at ~8% off, then standard monthly. Take rate on a 16% annual discount: 10–15% on a sales page, ~30% if it's the default option, 35–40% on the phone. Tip: sell a 6–12-week program first, then upsell the prepaid year. | 10–15% |
| 6 | **Round up** | 7s become 9s and add .99. His gym went $47 → $49.99 etc. (+6–11%) with no change in close rate. Skip for luxury goods, which use round numbers. | 1–3% |
| 7 | **Annual renewal fee on top of monthly** | Charge 1–3x the monthly rate once a year, framed as "rate protection" or "cancellation-fee prepayment". $39/month + $99/year comes to an effective $47/month (+20%). Drop it if they balk. Having both a setup fee and a renewal fee lets you waive one. | ~10% |
| 8 | **Automatic continuity ("continued access")** | Bolt a low-work, high-margin version (support, community, access, warranty) onto every front-end sale at 5–20% of the main price. It starts automatically after X time and is agreed upfront, never hidden. In his example LTV went $5,600 → $7,400 (+32%). | ~10% |
| 9 | **Ultra-high-ticket anchor** | Add a "mac daddy" option at 10x+ the core price and present it first. If only 10% take it, LTV goes $440 → $890, and the core offer looks cheaper so it closes more. Price it so you smile when someone buys. | 10–15% |
| 10 | **Paid guarantee / warranty upsell** | After the yes: "You just want the standard warranty on that?" Price it at 5–30% of the product (or at COGS so you can't lose). Half of buyers taking a 10% warranty covered roughly half his sales commissions. | 5–20% |

Combined estimated range: 26.8%–63.8% revenue lift, mostly profit (his estimates). The average US small business runs 7–10% net (Investopedia figure quoted in the playbook).
**Mistakes he calls out:** Hiding fees or continuity ("Don't be a sneak"). Changing too many things at once. Not having the guts: changing prices "requires guts".
**Ask the founder:** How do you bill now (cycle, card, contract)? Do you have a premium option? Do contracts allow annual increases?
**Pairs with:** Rules of Pricing · Price Raise Playbook · `03-money-models.md` (continuity, anchor upsell)
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Pricing

## Price Raise Playbook (raising prices on existing customers)
**Use when:** "How do I raise prices without losing clients?", "how do I tell existing customers?", "should I grandfather?", or a business is bleeding cash at the current price.
**Core idea:** Test on new customers, then roll out to existing ones with a short, value-first letter that gives them an *expiring* loyalty credit. Expect a small, temporary churn bump that mostly pulls forward cancellations that were coming anyway.
**How to run it:**
1. **Do the math first.** Know what % of customers you can lose and still make more money.
2. **Test on new customers.** Put new customers on the new price immediately. If conversion and churn hold, you have proof, and it tells existing customers the market already agreed.
3. **Segment** customers who joined before the raise.
4. **Write the RAISE letter:**
   - **R — Remind** them of value delivered, personalised with data: results, usage, wins.
   - **A — Address** the change in one direct sentence: "To keep investing in X for you, we're raising prices."
   - **I — Invest:** 3 bullets on what the money funds (people, training, equipment, tech, facilities, service levels), each tied to "more good stuff / less bad stuff" for them. Only list things you'll really do, and don't add new costs that eat the raise.
   - **S — Soften** with a loyalty reward: they keep the old rate for 3–6 months, shown as a dollar credit, then move to the new price.
   - **E — Explain away concerns:** "All replies come to me." PS for B2C: "if this materially affects your ability to buy groceries, let me know and we'll work something out." PS for B2B: "if this materially impacts your business…"
5. **Sign personally** (in ink for physical mail).
6. **Big jumps:** if the raise is ≥50%, also talk to people one-on-one. Or **stair-step** the discount off, e.g., $200 off for 4 months, then $100, then $50.
7. **Community businesses:** post a video version with comments off, and take questions privately.
8. **Incoming customers who heard it was cheaper:** "That's good news: it keeps getting better, [most] customers stayed, and this is the cheapest it'll ever be."
**Numbers & thresholds:**
- Raise prices **at least once a year**. Buffett raised See's Candies prices 50+ times over ~51 years, averaging ~10%/year and up to 17% in a single year (as told in the playbook).
- **Pair a raise with a launch** within 90 days. Frame it as early-adopter pricing.
- Shopify-competitor story: hundreds of customers moved from $30 to an average $3,000/month (100x). They lost one of ~300 (playbook).
- **What to expect:** three customer types: (1) see the value, grumble, stay; (2) actually affected, so extend their discount ~6 months; (3) were leaving anyway. Churn rises in month 1, drops below normal in month 2 and returns to baseline in month 3. That pattern means churn was pulled forward, not created.
- Inflation cost: $100 in 2017 money was worth ~$79 by 2024. A business at 20% margin that never repriced could be at ~0% (playbook).
**Quotes:**
> "Just because someone wants something for less doesn't mean they won't buy it for more." — $100M Playbook: Price Raise

**Mistakes he calls out:**
- **Grandfathering existing customers forever, or selling lifetime deals.** "Pay once, deliver forever" always gets fixed later, painfully. The exception is if delivery truly costs zero.
- Resenting early adopters for their low price. Early prices were fair when the product was worse.
- Waiting until the business is dying (Trey) instead of raising by choice.
- Long, apologetic letters. Keep it short.
**Ask the founder:** How big is the raise in %? What share of customers could you lose and break even? Have new customers already bought at the new price? Is there a launch in the next 90 days to pair it with? Who are the 10 accounts you'd call personally?
**Pairs with:** Pricing Philosophy · Rules of Pricing · Instant Profit Pricing Plays (#4 annual increases)
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Price Raise

## Early-Stage Price Laddering (first customers → full price)
**Use when:** zero to first ~20 customers, "what should my first price be?", or "how do I go from free to paid?"
**Core idea:** Start low to get proof and run water through the pipes, then step the price up on a fixed cadence until sales drop sharply. Then settle at the sweet spot.
**How to run it:**
1. Deliver free to a handful of people in exchange for feedback, results and reviews.
2. Use their results to sell paid customers: "Since I'm only taking 5 people, I can give you the attention you need for brag-worthy results, at 80% off, as long as you use it, give feedback and leave a review if it's deserved." (Leads-book warm outreach script, as presented in the Scaling Course.)
3. Raise ~20% every 5 sales: free → 80% off → 60% → 40% → 20% → full price, then keep going.
4. If someone paid "too much" before you found the ceiling, add bonuses or refund the difference.
**Numbers & thresholds:** **Cadence differs by source.** Scaling Course and $100M Leads warm outreach: raise every ~5 sales. LTV playbook: nudge ~20% every ~10 sales until sales drop dramatically. Use 5 when you have few deals, 10 when you have volume.
**Worked example (from source):** Gym Launch's package was raised over time: $6k → $8k → $10k → $12k → $16k → $20k → $22k → $30k → $35k → $50k (Scaling Course).
**Mistakes he calls out:** If you can't sell even the discounted rung, check (1) do you have proof that supports the claim, and are you showing it? (2) are you actually asking, with a script and conviction?
**Ask the founder:** How many paying customers do you have? What proof do you have? When did you last raise?
**Pairs with:** Rules of Pricing · Value Equation
**Sources:** Acquisition.com Scaling Course, Stage 1 "Monetize" · $100M Playbook: Lifetime Value · $100M Leads (warm outreach, via summaries)

## Fast Cash Play (cash from existing customers in ≤7 days)
**Use when:** "I need cash fast", "cash crunch / tax bill / payroll", "how do I make more from customers I already have?", or planning a quarterly promotion.
**Core idea:** A limited-time, limited-spot, ultra-premium offer sent to your warmest audience: current customers, past customers in good standing, and engaged leads. There's no extra acquisition cost and big tickets carry fat margins, so most of the cash is profit. **10x the 10%:** if 10% of customers pay 10x your price, revenue doubles.
> "Revenue is vanity. Profit is sanity. Cash is reality." — used to open $100M Playbook: Fast Cash

**How to run it:**
1. **List:** current customers, past customers in good standing, and engaged leads. Exclude leads you haven't contacted in 6+ months and warm them up for next time.
2. **Offer:** 1–3 core components plus 3 bonuses, built from *unscalable* value: 1:1 or many-on-1 attention from senior people, personalisation, convenience (24/7, extended hours), status, duration (6–12 months), speed (priority, <10-minute response), experience (white glove, personal cell), access (retreats, behind the scenes), network (meet top customers), secrets (vetted vendors, "black book").
3. **Price:** take your average transaction and add a zero, so 10x, up to 50x for maximum cash. Volume version: a prepaid year (monthly × 12) plus bonuses, which he "highly recommends" for existing bases.
   > "The only thing that should shock them as much as the price is the value that goes with it." — $100M Playbook: Fast Cash
4. **Cap:** 5–10% of customers so it sells out. Selling out makes the next one sell faster.
5. **Sales path:**
   - **Consult-based:** open purchase on day 1 and push people to book calls inside the 7-day window.
   - **Automated checkout:** build tension, open the cart to everyone at once, first come first served.
   - **Cards on file:** "Reply 'I'm in' and I'll charge the card on file."
6. **Sequence (consult version):**
   - −7 days: text that something's coming.
   - −6: announcement email with the core components and a price range ("four-figure investment").
   - −5: recap plus bonus #1.
   - −3: sales update, spots left.
   - −2: "X spots left" plus bonus #2.
   - −24 hours: best bonus, worth more than the price on its own.
   - −12 hours: sold out.
   - Back-out spot: **only if it's real.**
   Pair every email with a text.
7. **After:** deliver, then collect the **4Rs**: Reviews, Results, Referrals, Resells. Cool the list down with value, set the next date, and run it **every 90 days**.
**Numbers & thresholds:**
- Gym Launch tax-bill story: gyms sold a ~$5k offer to members. The *average* gym collected an extra ~$30k. Gyms that held $5k (instead of dropping to $3k) did $50k+.
- Early on, Fast Cash Plays were ~15% of his annual revenue but nearly half his profit.
- Target ≥90% gross margin on these plays.
- ROI benchmark (playbook): a $25k/month gym (166 members × $150, 25% margin, $75k/year net) running four quarterly plays adds $229k, taking net from $75k to $281k. *The playbook claims 22 new customers, but its line items sum to 8. Quote the cash, not the customer count.*
- If you haven't sold a substantial share by day 3–4, add to the offer before sending the rest of the sequence.
**Worked examples (from source):** B2C fitness "Total Transformation" year at $10k. B2B agency "Personal Brand Explosion" year at $50k.
**Mistakes he calls out:**
- "That's not scalable." It's not meant to be. The cash funds scaling.
- Running it weekly (fatigue) or yearly (leaving money). Quarterly is the sweet spot.
- Copying the cell-phone bonus literally. The point is extreme perceived value your customers specifically want.
- Selling a "huge discount" framing to new customers. Keep it to existing customers.
**Ask the founder:** How many current and past customers in good standing? Average transaction size? What unscalable thing do your best customers keep asking for? Can you deliver 5–10 of those in the next quarter?
**Pairs with:** Scarcity · Urgency · Bonuses · `03-money-models.md` (Continuity, Anchor Upsell)
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Fast Cash

## Offer Teardown Checklist (run on any offer the user pastes)
Run every line. Score each PASS / WEAK / FAIL. Report the **top 3 fixes ranked by expected profit impact**, then rewrite the offer.

**A. Market**
1. Who exactly is it for? Can you name the avatar in one line? Is it one avatar or several mixed together?
2. Massive pain? Purchasing power? Easy to target? Growing? Flag any FAIL on pain or purchasing power as fatal.

**B. Value Equation**
3. Is the dream outcome stated in buyer's words, specific, with status or ROI attached? Outcome or vehicle?
4. Likelihood: is there proof that looks like the buyer (numbers, recency, volume)? A mechanism? A guarantee?
5. Time delay: is there a first win within 72 hours? A stated time to full result?
6. Effort: what does the buyer have to do? Can any of it become DFY or DWY, or be templated?

**C. Construction**
7. Are the obstacles before, during and after purchase each answered by a named component? List the unanswered objections.
8. Is the delivery mix sensible (attention level, DIY/DWY/DFY, live/recorded, response speed)? Any high-cost, low-value parts to trim?
9. Is it a category of one, or can a buyer line it up feature by feature against a cheaper option?

**D. Enhancers**
10. Bonuses: does each one kill a specific objection? Are they valued? Does the total eclipse the core?
11. Guarantee: is the type matched to COGS and client control? Named? In the pitch?
12. Scarcity: a real cap stated? Urgency: a real deadline or cohort date?
13. Name: does it use 3+ of the MAGIC components?

**E. Price**
14. Value-based or cost/competitor-based? What multiple of the outcome's value is it?
15. Signals: close rate >50% (or >80%)? At capacity? No price complaints? Then it's under-priced.
16. Is there a premium or anchor option (≥10x core)? A downsell for unqualified buyers only?
17. Billing: longest cycle possible? One-time and ongoing separated? Annual option anchored first? 28-day cycle? Processing fee plus backup card? Annual increase clause?
18. Gross margin: ≥80% for services? If not, raise price or cut delivery cost.

**F. Cash & sequence (hand off to file 03)**
19. Does the first 30 days of gross profit cover 2× (CAC + COGS)? If not, which upsell, downsell or prepay closes the gap?
20. Is there continuity? Is there a quarterly Fast Cash play for the existing base?

**Output format:** (1) one-line verdict; (2) scorecard; (3) top 3 fixes with the math ("+X% close or +$Y per customer, illustrative unless they gave numbers"); (4) rewritten offer: name, promise, components, bonuses, guarantee, scarcity/urgency, price and billing; (5) 2–4 questions you still need answered.
**Sources:** synthesis of all cards above.
