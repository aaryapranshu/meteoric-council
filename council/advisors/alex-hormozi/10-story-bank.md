## Part 2 — Story cards

### A. Broke, early gyms, first lessons

## First gym: sleeping on the floor
**Story:** He says that when he opened his first gym he slept on the gym floor, had about $5,000 total, and the rent was $5,000. So he had one month to learn how to make money. He learned funnels from the internet and sold enough people in the first two weeks to grow to nine employees. He sent a photo to his dad, who had disapproved of him leaving the traditional path.
**Lesson he uses it for:** Constraint breeds skill. You don't need capital to start; you need to sell. His opening line in several videos is the contrast between sleeping on a gym floor and the later results.
**Fits when:** the founder is broke, scared to start, or thinks they need funding first.
**Confidence:** Self-told (the details of the "gym floor" have no primary document; the $1,036 balance in Dec 2016 is Verified).
**Sources:** YT "I Taught 116+ Salesmen My Closing Framework" (2021) · YT "How to Sell Better than 99% Of People" (2024) · YT "The Best SALES TRAINING On The Internet" (2022) · YT "If I Started A Business in 2026, I'd Do This" (2025)

## The Facebook slap → "attach a phone to the funnel"
**Story:** He says that soon after his first gym took off, a Facebook change raised his lead cost about 5× overnight and his marketing lost money every day. A gym-owner friend told him they had "attached the phone to the funnel": calling leads and selling an expensive front-end offer instead of cheap trials. He copied it. In his telling he went from losing money on the front end to roughly a 10:1 return, which meant he could pay ~$100 a lead instead of breaking even at ~$10.
**Lesson he uses it for:** When ad costs rise, fix monetisation (price, sales process, front-end offer) so you can outspend competitors. The business that makes the most per customer can pay the most to get one.
**Fits when:** "my ad costs went up", "ads stopped working", "I sell cheap trials and can't scale".
**Confidence:** Self-told.
**Sources:** YT "I Taught 116+ Salesmen My Closing Framework" (2021) · YT "How to Sell Better than 99% Of People" (2024)

## 300 flyers and a damaged Mercedes
**Story:** A mentor who ran tanning salons brought in thousands of customers each quarter by putting flyers with a free VIP card on cars. Hormozi put out 300 flyers. The phone rang once, and it was a man saying the flyer damaged his Mercedes. When he complained to the mentor, the mentor asked about his test size. The mentor tested with 5,000 flyers, then ran 5,000 a day for 30 days. Hormozi was comparing his 300 to the mentor's 150,000.
**Lesson he uses it for:** Volume negates luck. What looks like volatility or "it doesn't work" is usually low volume.
**Fits when:** "I tried ads/outreach/content and it didn't work", "sales are lumpy", "I posted 10 times".
**Confidence:** Self-told.
**Sources:** YT "How to Grow Your Business SO Fast in 2026 It Feels ILLEGAL" (2025)

## The charity launch: first 13 customers
**Story:** He says his first training clients came from an offer where people donated $500–$1,000 to a charity of their choice in exchange for 12 weeks of training. He got about 13 customers just from posts and DMs to people he knew. At the end he asked if they'd now pay him directly. Many said yes and referred others. His line: "I'm the charity now."
**Lesson he uses it for:** Start free or nearly free to get your first customers, proof and referrals. He repeated the pattern with Gym Launch turnarounds and ALAN's first customers.
**Fits when:** zero customers, no testimonials, launching a new product line or division.
**Confidence:** Self-told.

## "Big Booty Boot Camp"
**Story:** At his first gym he ran a six-week programme of deadlifts, squats and hip thrusts. **Lesson he uses it for:** Advertise the result, not the vehicle. Naming changes engagement more than people expect. Test names with small ad tests or by polling your audience.
**Fits when:** naming an offer, lead magnet or programme; "my offer sounds boring".

## Opening gyms with customers' money
**Story:** He describes pre-selling a new gym before it opened. In his example he spent about $5 a lead, closed 1 in 5 on a $500 programme (~$25 to acquire $500), and recycled the cash weekly into equipment, flooring and paint. He says that's how he got to six locations by his mid-20s, and that the constraint then became trainers and managers, not cash.
**Lesson he uses it for:** Client-financed acquisition and a fast cash conversion cycle. If customers pay you before you pay for delivery, cash stops being the constraint.
**Fits when:** "I need funding", "I can't afford ads", cash-flow problems, opening a location.

## The 5:30am broken pipe
**Story:** When he'd finally stopped sleeping at his first gym, a trainer called him at 5:30am saying he had bad news. He assumed someone had died. It was a leaking water pipe. The trainer hadn't called a plumber or found a bucket; he'd called Hormozi. Hormozi told him to do both.

### B. Gym Launch

## Gym Launch 1.0: turnarounds on the road, for free

## Gym Launch licensing: the 100:1 year
**Story:** He switched to licensing (teaching gym owners his system instead of flying in). He says that in the first year he spent about $100K on ads and made about $10M, a 100:1 return. ## The Gym Launch value math
**Story:** To explain how to frame value, he says the average gym made about $30,000 in its first 30 days on the system. Meanwhile the average gym owner took home about $36,000 a year. So the programme could deliver close to a year's take-home pay in a month.
**Lesson he uses it for:** Quantify the dream outcome against the buyer's current reality. When the result is worth multiples of the price, the price stops mattering.

## The theme park on 14 days of cash (Boulder Adventure Park)
**Story:** Paul ran an indoor family entertainment centre in Grand Prairie, Texas. Total cost was $22M ($8M raised, the rest bank debt). It did about $4.8M a year, unprofitably: about $400K a month against a $550K break-even, with ~$180K a month of debt service, and about 14 days of runway. Hormozi's first step was to generate cash. They bet on one offer: a summer camp sold to the email list, then a giveaway with a 7-day countdown (first prize a whole summer free, second prize a large discount, a 72-hour window to claim, referral entries), followed by phone calls and a "buy four weeks, get six free" style offer. Result per Paul: 3,700 leads in a local market, best-ever cost per lead, and summer camps up 325% on the prior year. The leads exceeded his small sales team's capacity. Admissions doubled over the following 4–6 weeks.
**Lesson he uses it for:** In a cash crisis, make one bet and pull cash forward. Expect a third to half of launch leads in the final 48 hours. When lead flow exceeds the sales team, hire salespeople: that's low-risk growth spend. Repeat a 10:1 play (here, for birthdays) as often as it works. Some entrepreneurship is just buying the next month of runway.
**Fits when:** cash crunch, seasonal business, local or brick-and-mortar, "I have a list but no sales", giveaway or launch design.
**Confidence:** Self-told (on-camera case study; results reported by the owner).
**Sources:** YT "Can I Save This Failing Theme Park in 90 Days?" (2026)
