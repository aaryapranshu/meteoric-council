## Hook → Retain → Reward
**Use when:** any piece of content or ad: "how do I structure a post/video/reel?"
**Core idea:** Every piece of content has three jobs. **Hook:** earn attention (first ~3 seconds, the headline, the thumbnail). **Retain:** keep it with lists, steps or stories, using open loops ("way number one… two… three"). **Reward:** deliver on the promise so they feel the time was worth it, ideally something share-worthy (a laugh, a twist) or an action. An ad has the same shape, except the reward is replaced by a **call to action**.
**How to run it:**
1. Write the hook last-minute? No. Write the hook *first* and spend most of your time there. The hook dictates the content, not the other way round.
2. Retain with structure: state step 1 → why it works → step 2 → why… Or a story with an unresolved question.
3. Reward: answer the promise in full, then (content) a share-worthy close or (ad) one clear CTA.
4. Optional: shift "how to" framing to "how I" (book via secondary): it shows you did it.
**Numbers & thresholds:** He calls the first 3 seconds the most important of any video.
**Worked example (from source, his trash-company demo):** Hook: a question about getting your spouse to take the trash out. Retain: two tips, each with a reason. Reward: a joke CTA to call his service.
**Mistakes he calls out:** burying the payoff; tons of production with no structure; content with no reward, so nobody shares.
**Ask the founder:** What's the promise in the first line? What structure (list, steps, story) carries it? What do they get at the end?
**Pairs with:** Hooks cards; Proof-Promise-Plan Intros; Content Strategy.
**Sources:** YT "The Business Model That's So Simple, Anyone Can Try It" (2023) · Research notes ($100M Leads: hook-retain-reward)

## What a Hook Is (and why it gets 80% of the effort)
**Use when:** "my content/ads/emails get ignored", "low CTR", "low open rates".
**Core idea:** A hook is whatever the audience sees or hears first: first line, first seconds, image, sound, subject line. It has two parts:
1. **Callout:** makes the right person think "this is for me" (the cocktail-party effect: you hear your own name through noise). It can be verbal ("Local business owners…") or visual (a prop, setting or look that signals the avatar).
2. **Condition for value:** an explicit or implied promise: *if you [the target] consume this, you get [value]*. People consume if they expect the benefit to beat the cost of their time.
Rule of thumb from the Hooks playbook: "If you have ten hours to advertise, focus your first eight hours on the hooks."
**How to run it:**
1. Make the callout specific enough to get the right people and broad enough to get as many of them as possible.
2. Use verbal and nonverbal hooks together where the platform allows.
3. Watch competitors' long-running ads aimed at your avatar. If an ad runs for months, its callout works. Log them.
**Numbers & thresholds:**
- His claim: good hooks can 5x, 10x, sometimes 100x the people who pay attention, for the same effort.
- He spends ~10x the effort on the first 5 seconds: ~55 of 60 minutes (2024).
- Portfolio example: changing only the landing-page headline lifted booking rate 62% with opt-in rate unchanged.
- Dean Graziosi's story (in Dean's words in the playbook, retold by Hormozi on YouTube; details differ): re-shooting only the opening lines of a Larry King infomercial turned a flop into a hit. The playbook says the fixed infomercial went on to sell millions of books; the 2024 video says ~$100M in sales after a $250k flop.
**Mistakes he calls out:** an ego-driven opener about you instead of them (in the Dean story, the audience didn't care who he was); vague hooks; spending hours on the body and minutes on the first line.
**Ask the founder:** Who exactly should stop scrolling? What are they promised in the first line? Would they say "this is for me"?
**Pairs with:** Hook Types; 70-20-10; Write Me 10 Hooks.
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Hooks · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024) · YT "How to Grow Your Business SO Fast in 2026…" (2025)

## Hook Types (verbal and nonverbal)
**Use when:** generating variety; "give me different angles".
**Core idea:** Seven verbal formats cover almost all of his winners. Nonverbal hooks (a visual, a prop, a sound, an odd action like catching a banana) run alongside them.

| Type | Pattern | Example (illustrative, AI-agency niche) |
|---|---|---|
| **Label** | Name the avatar | "Agency owners, I made you something." |
| **Question** (yes or open) | Ask what they'd say yes to, or a choice | "Would you rather hire 3 ops people or never hire them?" |
| **Conditional** | If [situation], then [result/lesson/command] | "If your team copies data between tools, you're paying people to be robots." |
| **Command** | Tell them to do something | "Watch this before you hire your next VA." |
| **Statement** | Bold claim or promise | "The fastest-growing firms I've seen hire *after* they automate." |
| **List / steps** | Number + topic | "7 tasks every 20-person company should automate first." |
| **Story / narrative** | Drop into a scene | "Last Tuesday a client's invoice bot caught a $40k error…" |
| **Exclamation / provocation** | Strong emotion | "This is the most expensive habit in your business." |

**Numbers & thresholds:** His 121 best hooks of one year (ads, content, email, X), bucketed: statements 47.9%, commands 21.5%, questions 9.9%, exclamations 9.1%, lists 5.8%, stories 3%, conditionals 2.5%. (Labels aren't a separate bucket in that chart.) Default to statements and commands; use the rest for variety.
**Mistakes he calls out:** only one format; words without visuals on video platforms.
**Pairs with:** Levels of Awareness; Write Me 10 Hooks.

## Finding Hooks: 70-20-10 and the Swipe File
**Use when:** "I don't know what to say", "how do I come up with hooks?", "how much should I experiment?".
**Core idea:** Allocate effort like Google's 70-20-10. **70% proven** hooks (your past winners and others' winners). **20% winner-adjacent** (model a format working in another niche: "Use this X formula to Y" → your topic). **10% new** (wild ideas). Losers from the 10% get documented so you don't repeat them; winners move into the 70%.
**How to run it:**
1. **Build a hook spreadsheet:** one tab per platform; columns Name / Hook / Views (or conversions) / Link. Review your all-time best before every content or ad session.
2. **Sources, in priority order (GOATed Ads):** (1) your previous winning ads; (2) hooks from your own top free content (shorts, longs, tweets, emails), cut off and reused on ads; (3) other people's ads you save (keep a phone album); (4) others' high-view content in your space; (5) platform ad libraries (last: you can't tell what's actually winning). Winning hooks in one industry often work in others.
3. **Outlier hunting:** use outlier tools, or search your topics on each platform. Paste screenshot + 2–3 repackaging ideas into a deck.
4. **Cross-platform reuse:** a top X post becomes a short's hook or a caption.
5. **First time with no data:** collect 50 favourite hooks from top performers, use all of them, then see what wins. In his words: "Let the data do the teaching." The top 10% of even bad content shows you what to make more of.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Two versions of the split exist. Hooks playbook and paid-ads Q&A (2024): 70/20/10. The 2024 marketing video: of 50 hooks, 40 proven (80%) and 10 new (20%). Same principle: most effort on what's proven.
**Mistakes he calls out:** most people flip it (a little proven, lots of "creative" new); believing you're more creative than the data.
**Pairs with:** Hook Testing; GOATed Ad Assembly.
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Hooks · $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024) · YT "Learn Paid Ads in 30 Minutes!" (2024)

## Hook Testing
**Use when:** "which hook is best?", "how do I test?"
**Core idea:** Don't guess. Record many hooks per body and let performance pick.
**How to run it:**
1. **Ads:** record ~10 hooks for every piece of ad body (10x). In GOATed Ads: ~50 hooks per weekly session.
2. **Long-form video:** record 3–4 alternative openings; the team picks in post.
3. **Email:** split-test every campaign; log winners in a separate file.
4. **Each session after the first:** take the top 10% (top 5 hooks) → 70% of the next batch reuses them → 20% are permutations (words *and* visuals) → 10% are completely new.
5. Measure by the outcome that matters (conversions for ads, opens/clicks for email, the right viewers for content), not just views.
**Mistakes he calls out:** one hook per ad; testing bodies before hooks; declaring a format "dead" after one weak hook.
**Pairs with:** What to Measure; Kaleidoscope.
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Hooks (Hook Checklist; one line of the testing list is truncated in the source copy) · $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads

## Proof → Promise → Plan (intros for educational content)
**Use when:** long-form video, webinar, podcast or post intros; retention drops in the first 30 seconds.
**Core idea:** Entertainment intros confirm the thumbnail. Education intros must answer "why should I listen to you?" He rewatched his top 35 educational videos and found three elements, in any order (lead with the strongest):
- **Proof:** evidence you've done it (results, numbers, credentials).
- **Promise:** what they'll get from this piece.
- **Plan:** how the piece will deliver it ("six changes, one by one").
He later calls proof-promise-plan his hook formula for content, ads and outbound (2025).
**How to run it:** one line of each, ~15 seconds total. Make it true and specific.
**Worked example (illustrative):** "We've automated 40,000 hours of admin for 30 companies [proof]. In 8 minutes you'll know the three processes to automate first [promise]. I'll show each one with the real numbers [plan]."
**Mistakes he calls out:** assuming viewers know you; curiosity-only titles that don't match the content.
**Pairs with:** Content Strategy (assume nothing); SPCL Influence.
**Sources:** YT "How I Gained 7.8 Million Followers In 40 Months" (2024) · YT "How to Grow Your Business SO Fast in 2026…" (2025)

## Levels of Awareness & Expansion Hooks
**Use when:** "ads worked then capped out", "CAC rises when we scale spend", "we've saturated the market".
**Core idea:** Ads start profitably on the warmest, smallest audience and get less efficient as they reach colder, larger ones (Eugene Schwartz's five levels, which Hormozi treats as a continuum). A spend ceiling usually means your hooks only speak to the warm levels. To grow, write hooks for colder levels.

| Level | Hook driver | Template (from playbook structure) | Illustrative B2B example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most aware (past customers, list) | **Offer** | "[Deal] on [product] until [date]" | "Clients: 2 extra automation builds free if you renew by Friday." |
| Product aware | **Proof** | "Why [N] [avatar] chose [you] for [problem]" | "Why 30 ops leaders picked us to kill manual reporting." |
| Solution aware | **Promise** | "The fastest way to [result] — [you]" | "Cut admin hours in half in 60 days, without new software." |
| Problem aware | **Pain** | "Tired of [specific problem]? There's a better way." | "Still hiring people to copy-paste between tools?" |
| Unaware | **Curiosity** | "The hidden [thing] costing you [money/time]" | "The invisible job your best employee does 10 hours a week." |

**How to run it:** sort your current hooks by level. If ~90% are "most aware", spread the next 50 across levels. When in doubt, go a little broader: you keep the warm audience and pick up some cold. Memes (general, or specific to your audience's culture) are the widest hooks.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Density of buyers per 1,000 eyeballs falls as you go colder (his "watermelon" image: seeds thick at the tip). Expect ratios like ~20:1 warm, falling to ~5:1 then ~3:1 at 100x spend, and still more absolute profit (his illustrative numbers). "The bigger the plane, the longer the runway": colder audiences and higher prices need more selling.
**Mistakes he calls out:** offer-driven hooks to a cold national audience for an unknown product (only giant known brands, e.g. a $5 footlong, can do that); a warm message to a cold audience; blaming the market.
**Ask the founder:** At what daily spend does CAC jump? Which awareness level are most hooks written for? What pain does the problem-aware buyer feel before they know you exist?
**Pairs with:** GOATed Ad Assembly; LTGP:CAC in file 04 (Running Paid Ads).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024)

## GOATed Ad Assembly (hooks × meat × CTAs)
**Use when:** "how many ads should I make?", "how do I make ads faster?", "ads hit a wall at $X/day".
**Core idea:** You hit a wall of ad *quality*, not market size. Better ads come from making far more ads, and you do that by building them in parts on an assembly line. In the GOATed Ads playbook's words: "I wasn't really creating ads at all. I was assembling them."
**How to run it:**
1. **Weekly ad day** (his portfolio advice: every Friday). Cram the morning of: review your swipe file and historical winners (first 10–30 seconds of each).
2. **Prep split:** ~80% of prep on hooks, ~20% on meat, ~0% on CTAs. Ninety percent of advertising is prep, not recording.
3. **Assemble:** **50 hooks × 3–5 meats × 1–3 CTAs = 150–750 ads per week.** Roughly 1–2 hours writing hooks, ~1 hour recording meats, ~5 minutes recording CTAs.
4. Hooks: half from past winners (bread-and-butter), plus expansion hooks across awareness levels.
5. Meat: pick formats from the next card. Bodies rotate less often (fewer people see them), so 3–5 a week is plenty.
6. CTA: 1–3, tested by running identical hook + meat to the same audience with only the CTA changed. Keep the winner.
7. Run, find the outliers, squeeze them (Kaleidoscope), repeat.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Portfolio weight-loss company hit a wall at $5–8k/day spend, making ~5 ads a month. Plan: 5/month → ~600/month. Result: doubled in two quarters (his account). He made ~35 ads/week when scaling his own company 5x. Side benefit: competitors can't tell which of your ads is winning.
**Mistakes he calls out:** "We record once a month"; treating every ad as a one-off creation; not tagging and reusing parts.
**Ask the founder:** How many *new* ads did you launch last week? Where's your hook bank? Which ad is your current control?
**Pairs with:** Ad Meat Formats; CTA Rules; Kaleidoscope; Ad Script Template.
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024)

## Ad Meat Formats (the body)
**Use when:** "what kind of ad should I make?", stuck on creative.
**Core idea:** The meat fulfils the hook. It educates about the offer, product, solution or problem at the hook's awareness level. Five formats worked for him across services, education, physical products, local and software. Mix them.

| Format | Variants | Notes from source |
|---|---|---|

## CTA Rules
**Use when:** "people watch but don't click", "what do I say at the end?"
**Core idea:** Clear beats clever. Interest gives huge motivation for a tiny window, so tell people *exactly* what to do, and ideally *show* them doing it.
**How to run it:** a complete CTA shows or tells:
1. **What** to do and **how** ("tap the button below")
2. **When** ("before Friday")
3. **What they get** ("and you'll get the free audit")
4. **What happens next** (optional, but key for lead magnets and multi-step funnels: "you'll pick a time on the next page")
5. **Demonstrate it:** screen-record or walk through the click → form → thank-you page so they get exactly what they expect.
Layer urgency, scarcity, bonuses or guarantees on top when available. Any reason to act now beats no reason. Content CTAs should offer personalisation ("want me to apply this to your business?"), not "more stuff" (file 04).
**Numbers & thresholds:** A sound CTA has never broken a campaign; *no* CTA has (his claim). Test 3 CTA phrasings with an identical hook + meat, then stick with the winner.
**Mistakes he calls out:** no CTA; clever CTAs; asking once; a CTA that doesn't match the landing page.
**Pairs with:** Ad Script Template; Lead Magnets (file 04).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads · YT "Watch This To Generate 1000s of Leads (In Any Niche)" (2025)

## Kaleidoscope: Squeeze the Winners
**Use when:** "we finally have a winning ad", "creative fatigue", "how do I scale a winner?"
**Core idea:** Results follow a power law: a few ads make most of the money (he says the top 5% make 95% of income). When one wins, don't move on. Make dozens to hundreds of variations of it. His team spends ~80% of creative resources reskinning winners (2024 Q&A). (GOATed Ads says the full kaleidoscope method is a separate step; the levers below come from his videos.)
**How to run it — variation levers:**
1. Background colour or backdrop
2. Props or on-screen visuals
3. Re-enact it (same script, re-recorded, maybe a different shirt)
4. Reorder the sections
5. Visual filters (black-and-white, sepia, contrast)
6. Visual effects
7. Fonts and captions
8. Pacing (1.2x–1.5x, or slowed sections)
9. Music
10. Same hook with a new body. Or slice the first 3 seconds of top winners onto 100+ other ads. Also new headlines, subheads and ad copy.
**Numbers & thresholds:** He made ~100 versions of one winning customer testimonial ad (the "Kale/Cale" gym-owner ad; spelling varies in captions). New customers enter the market daily, so it's still the first time *they* see it.
**Mistakes he calls out:** getting bored of your own winner (Henry Ford story: tired of an ad campaign that hadn't launched yet); chasing new concepts while a winner still has runway.
**Pairs with:** GOATed Ad Assembly; What to Measure.
**Sources:** YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024) · YT "Learn Paid Ads in 30 Minutes!" (2024) · $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads

## Proof in Ads
**Use when:** "ads aren't converting", "what should my ads be about?", "should I be in my ads?"
**Core idea:** When he sorted five years of Gym Launch ads, about 80% of the winners had no educational content, weren't focused on the offer, didn't star him, and told one story: *this product works*. The losers talked about the company and its "unique mechanism". In the Proof Checklist's words: "Your promise is not a differentiator. Your proof is." Anyone can copy a claim; nobody can copy your results.
**How to run it:**
1. **Claim your proof, don't prove your claim.** Collect data first, then make the claims it supports. If the data is weak, fix reality (free work, better product) before the ads.
2. **Make proof more compelling** (ad-relevant items from his checklist): in-person > virtual; live > recorded; **raw > processed** (an unedited phone testimonial beat the edited cut of the same story); show > tell; others saying it > you saying it; **identical to them > opposite** (ads with female gym owners brought female gym owners, and older brought older, so represent each segment); personal and specific > generic; big result > small > none; newer > older; more > less; third-party verified > not; numbers > no numbers; metaphors > jargon.
3. **State data claims in four parts:** what % of people (or average/median) → achieve X → in Y time / after Z attempts → under these conditions. Fewer conditions = more compelling.
4. Be specific on the negatives (the pain), vaguer on the positives. It's more compliant and sets realistic expectations.
5. Proof wins in local and cold markets especially: let clients talk; the founder steps back.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Examples of data claims from his businesses (July 2024): "54.1% of people who start a paid community on Skool make money"; "the average Gym Lord adds over $100,000 profit in their first complete year". He also discloses the bottom 20% (~$40k extra revenue, roughly breakeven). These figures are time-bound.
**Mistakes he calls out:** making claims bigger than your proof ("claim wars"); 10-year-old faceless testimonials; ads all about you.
**Ask the founder:** What's your best measured result, and what % of clients get it? Do you have raw video from clients who look like your buyers? What can you show live?
**Pairs with:** Marketing Machine (file 04); proof section of the sales file (`05` Proof Checklist); Ad Meat (testimonial/faceless).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Proof Checklist · $100M Playbook: Marketing Machine · YT "Learn Paid Ads in 30 Minutes!" (2024) · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024)

## What to Measure (ads and content)
**Use when:** "are my ads working?", "what metric should I watch?", "views are up but sales are down".

| Question | What he measures / the rule |
|---|---|
| Is the business allowed to scale ads? | LTGP:CAC. Floor ~3:1 (book); ~6–9:1 for human-heavy businesses (podcast refinement, secondary). CAC recovered within 30 days if possible (file 04). |
| Where to optimise first? | **Ads: front to back.** Click-through can go from ~1% to ~4% by fixing the front end (a 4x on the whole funnel). A back-end fix like show rate 50%→75% is "only" 1.5x. **Business: back to front.** LTV sets how much you can spend. |
| Which hook won? | The first 3 seconds of top 5–10% ads. Reuse them on other bodies. |
| Is a new channel progressing? | Step by step: clicks → opt-ins → right people → shows → sales. Walk the dollar across the bridge over 3–12 months. |
| Are leads good? | Too many bad leads → add good friction; too few → remove friction; bad with no friction → targeting/message. |
| Is content working? | Revenue, opt-ins, applications and sales, not views. He uses UTMs on description links. On YouTube he found ad revenue (RPM × views) a fast proxy for *who* is watching: high-value viewers raise RPM. A 90-day experiment of broader content doubled or tripled views but halved ad revenue and cut book sales, leads and applications. |
| Is a viewer metric lying? | The algorithm shows what the *most* people like, not what the *most valuable* people like. Watch IRL signals, e.g. texts from the buyers you want. |

**Mistakes he calls out:** CPL in isolation; views as the north star; changing the back end when the front end is the 4x lever.

## Content Strategy: Volume, Narrow, Education
**Use when:** "what should I post?", "how often?", "content gets views but no clients", "should I make broader content to grow?"
**Core idea:** Content for a business (not a media company) should attract *buyers*, not views. Make a lot of it, narrow it to your best avatar, educate rather than entertain, and give your best stuff away.
**How to run it:**
1. **Volume first.** Most founders post 1/day or less. Acquisition.com reports ~35,000 pieces/year and 50+ pieces/day. Volume is close to linear with prospects. Aim for *quality volume*: he names the middle ground between "make good stuff" and "make lots of stuff" internally ("qualume", spelling uncertain from captions).
2. **Pick the buyer, then the topic.** Study your top 20% customers (psychographics, geography, demographics, actions). Make content solving *their* problems. The content itself does the targeting: algorithms read what you say and show it to people interested in it.
3. **Education > edutainment > entertainment** for business. His data: entertainment viewers want more entertainment; education viewers want more education. One rarely converts into the other. Education's job is to change behaviour.
4. **Narrow > wide.** Two experiments (one quarter of broader content; 90 days of edutainment) raised views and *lowered* book sales, leads and portfolio applications. He went back to "hardcore business".
5. **Give away the secrets, sell the implementation.** He calls this a well-known saying (not his coinage) and adds: make your free stuff better than their paid stuff. Give away the low-ticket thing too if it's a headache. ~99% of people who consume your free stuff never buy, so your reputation is built by the free stuff. Prospects use free results as a preview of paid results.
6. **"Vertical value":** broad pieces should still be valuable to both a beginner and a $100M operator. Advanced, narrow pieces get fewer views but more revenue.
7. **CTA every piece** toward a lead magnet or call, with tracking.
**Numbers & thresholds:** 9% of US adults own a business; ~5% of those exceed $1M revenue; ~1 in 250 exceeds $10M (as he cites Census data). Set view expectations by your real market size. His 2025–26 claims: 3B impressions in a year; top-viewed videos made no sales while lower-view operator videos made the most revenue.
**Worked example (illustrative):** AI agency targeting 20–100-person ops-heavy firms. Instead of "AI news" reels: weekly teardown "We automated [client]'s invoice approvals: 14 hours/week saved, here's the exact flow" + CTA "Want us to map yours? 3 audit slots this week."
**Mistakes he calls out:** chasing views/likes (ego metrics); trend-hacking the news instead of "your news"; judging yourself against entertainers' view counts.
**Ask the founder:** Who are your top-20% customers and what do they struggle with? How many pieces a week? Which pieces produced a sale? Would you charge for your free content?
**Pairs with:** Do Epic Stuff; Assume Nothing; Branding; Marketing Machine (file 04).

## Do Epic Stuff, Talk About What You Did (the "document" question)
**Use when:** "I don't know what to post", "imposter syndrome", "should I document my journey?", "how do I build a personal brand?"
**Core idea:** His two-step content model: **do epic stuff, then talk about what you did.** "How I" beats "how to": you aren't telling anyone what to do, just reporting what happened, which sidesteps judgment and carries built-in proof. Out-work people first; the outcomes then give you the shorter, stronger headlines. Say what only you can say; show what only you can show.
**Verification note:** "Document, don't create" is a phrase popularised by other creators (commonly attributed to Gary Vaynerchuk); it doesn't appear in the Hormozi sources read. His closest positions: documenting along the way is fine provided you state the facts and tell the truth (2026). And his 2026 method is to *capture* rather than *create*: transcribe meetings and have AI pull the most interesting real moments; engineer proof into delivery; do free audits and document them.
**How to run it:**
1. List what you did this week: client wins, experiments, numbers, mistakes. That's the content calendar.
2. Turn each into "here's what we did, here's what happened, here's what I'd do again".
3. Demonstrate instead of claiming (play the real call, run the demo live, clean the stain in front of them).
4. Value per second, not seconds of value: compress long experiences into short pieces (his example: 13 years in one video).
5. **Tell the whole truth, including the bad parts.** He calls imposter syndrome the shame of misrepresenting yourself. The fix is honesty ("I was bad at running an agency, great at selling for one, so I do the selling"). His point: the truth, told in full, is the highest-converting message.
**Mistakes he calls out:** wanting to say epic things without having done any; making up stories; hiding failures (people only believe the good parts when you show the bad ones).
**Ask the founder:** What did you actually do or ship this month? What result can you demonstrate on camera? What's true about your story that you've been hiding?
**Pairs with:** Proof in Ads; SPCL Influence.
**Sources:** YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024) · YT "My Actual Social Media Strategy For 2027 - The Algorithm" (2026) · YT "The New Way of Making Content In The Age of AI" (2026)

## Assume Nothing; Remind More Than Teach
**Use when:** "I've already said everything", "should I repost?", "do I introduce myself every time?"
**Core idea:** Make every piece work for a stranger, and repeat your best ideas forever. As he puts it, "we need to be reminded more than we need to be taught" (YT, 2024).
**How to run it:**
1. **Title for strangers:** say who you are and why it matters in the title ("$200M/yr CEO…", not your name alone).
2. **Introduce yourself and why they should listen** every time. Warm fans like the reminder; cold viewers need it. Keep a consistent intro with small variations (TV theme-song logic).
3. **Explain every reference.** Inside jokes leave newcomers outside, and people spend money on the inside.
4. **Repost greatest hits.** Test: repost last year's #1 piece; he bets it does as well or better, because the audience has grown.
5. **Same idea, new formats and contexts:** written, audio, course, whiteboard; applied to services, products, software. Same concept, different names in the story (like the news).
**Mistakes he calls out:** assuming everyone consumes everything (he's grateful if someone watches one piece a week out of 50+/day); getting bored of your own material first.
**Pairs with:** Kaleidoscope (same logic for ads); Branding (repeat the associations).
**Sources:** YT "How I Gained 7.8 Million Followers In 40 Months" (2024) · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024) · YT "The Business Model That's So Simple, Anyone Can Try It" (2023)

## SPCL: How Content Builds Influence
**Use when:** "how do I become an authority?", "people watch but don't act", personal brand strategy.
**Core idea:** He defines influence as a high likelihood of compliance with requests (watch next, opt in, buy). Four stackable elements (2025):
- **Status:** you control resources people want (money, access, results).
- **Power:** "say-do correspondence": you gave instructions, they followed, something good happened. Each cycle makes the next request likelier. If he had to pick one, it's power.
- **Credibility:** third-party verification (e.g. he paid Guinness judges to verify his book-launch records).
- **Likeness:** they relate to you (values or literally look like you). Be yourself; there's zero ROI in acting like an archetype.
**How to run it:** in each piece, show status (results), give followable instructions that work (power), add third-party proof (credibility), and be unscripted (likeness). Prefer formats with more time per person: shorts lead to longs, longs to live. His 2025–26 strategy: "live" and "interactive". Influence is domain-specific (he wouldn't have it on fashion).
**Numbers & thresholds:** 120 minutes of long-form ≈ 480 fifteen-second shorts of exposure (his math). Time-bound shift: in 2024 he said shorts and long viewers are mostly separate audiences, and longs drove more conversions (book sales, opt-ins, applications). By 2025 he frames shorts as a way to get people to "risk their time" on a long, with live streams producing the most influence.
**Pairs with:** Do Epic Stuff; Trust Economy card; Branding.
**Sources:** YT "My Actual Social Media Strategy For 2026" (2025) · YT "How I Gained 7.8 Million Followers In 40 Months" (2024)

## Content in the Trust Economy (AI-era strategy)
**Use when:** "AI can make content now, does it still matter?", "should I use AI avatars?", "how do I stand out?"
**Core idea:** AI disrupts low-stakes creators first (memes, comedy, low-risk B2C tips). The higher the stakes of following advice (money, business, health), the more audiences demand real-world proof and live demonstration, which AI can't easily fake yet. We are in show-me times, not tell-me times (his framing).
**How to run it:**
1. Shift content toward demonstrated expertise: real client situations, many varied avatars, live problem-solving (calls, Q&As, audits on camera).
2. Engineer proof into delivery (the "self-licking ice cream cone": products and services generate marketing, which gets customers, who generate more marketing). See Marketing Machine in file 04.
3. Use AI for capture and editing (transcribe meetings, find moments), not for fake stories.
4. **Run production like Elon's "algorithm"** (2026 video, after the book *The Algorithm*): (1) question requirements ("What does that mean? How do you know? So what?"); (2) delete steps (e.g. he cut colour grading: no evidence it mattered); (3) simplify (two videos at 95% beat one at 100%); (4) accelerate (who-what-when, daily check-ins); (5) automate *last*. Litmus test: since using AI, are views, leads and revenue actually up?
5. An ounce of pre-work beats a pound of post: ~¼ of the old editing time moved into research cut ~90–95% of post-production (2024).
**Mistakes he calls out:** automating steps that shouldn't exist; heavy production that distracts from the message (in his last-30-days review, 9 of the top 12 reels emphasised language; 8 of the bottom 12 emphasised effects); rising AI token bills with flat revenue.
**Pairs with:** Proof in Ads; Marketing Machine (file 04); ops/AI file if present.
**Sources:** YT "The New Way of Making Content In The Age of AI" (2026) · YT "My Actual Social Media Strategy For 2027 - The Algorithm" (2026) · YT "How I Gained 7.8 Million Followers In 40 Months" (2024)

## Platform Approach
**Use when:** "which platform?", "should I be on all of them?"
- **Start:** one platform where your buyer is and your skills fit (file 04, Core Four). Post at volume there first (More, Better, New).
- **Then everywhere:** his original three-step plan was to make as much good content as possible, post it on every platform, and learn as fast as possible (2024). Reuse winners across platforms (X post → short hook → caption → email).
- **Formats:** long-form builds the most trust per viewer; shorts get discovery; live gets the most influence (see SPCL for the 2024→2025 shift).
- **Packaging per platform:** clear over curious thumbnails and titles (vague thumbnails lowered satisfaction because viewers didn't get what they expected). Language on screen > visual effects.
- **Email** is still one of the most-checked channels (file 04).
- **Paid** usually starts on Meta for targeting ease; also retargeting everywhere + own-brand search (file 04).

## What a Brand Is (his operational definition)
**Use when:** "should I invest in brand?", "what is branding, really?", "brand vs direct response?"
**Core idea:** Most brand definitions describe feelings but don't say what to *do*. His definition is behavioural: "Branding means pairing two or more things together" (Branding playbook). Customers pair you with whatever you say, do, look like and appear next to.
- **Good branding** = pairing your business with things most of your ideal audience *likes*.
- **Bad branding** = pairing with things they *dislike*.
- **No branding** = exposure doesn't change behaviour; you're invisible.
- **Advertising vs branding:** advertising lets people know the cow belongs to *someone*; branding lets them know it belongs to *you* and what that means. Good ads + bad brand = everyone knows you suck.
**Measure it:**
- **Reach** (advertising output: how many know you)
- **Influence** (chance seeing you changes behaviour)
- **Direction** (toward or away, which depends on the audience: the same politician pulls one group and repels another)
For a business the scoreboard is money: the ideal audience likes it *and* buys.
**Why it matters:** brand is how you lower CAC (convert more ad viewers; launch new products to a ready audience), raise LTV (charge 2–10x for the same thing) and lower risk (repeat buying without comparing competitors). He quotes Buffett: pricing power is the single most important factor in evaluating a business.
**Myths he corrects:**
- "No such thing as bad publicity": wrong. His line: "Good beats bad. Bad beats none. Lots of good beats everything."
- "You must be polarising": not required. Strong positive brands exist; selling polarises people anyway, so don't manufacture it.
- Pepsi × Kendall Jenner protest ad: good advertising, bad branding (paired the product with something most of its audience didn't find joyful), sales hurt, pulled within days.
**Numbers & thresholds:** The playbook's branded vs unbranded example (same $500 COGS and $2,000 ad spend; branded gets higher CTR, conversion and price) is directionally clear. But its figures are internally inconsistent (unbranded price shown as $2,500 and $2,000; "50% more customers" vs 2 → 18 sales). Use it as illustration only.
**Ask the founder:** What do your ideal customers already like and trust? What are you currently seen next to (podcasts, partners, clients, topics)? Does the product deliver the promise?
**Pairs with:** Building Brand Associations; Proof in Ads; pricing file (pricing power).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Branding

## Building Brand Associations (start, grow, pivot)
**Use when:** "how do I build a brand from zero?", "how do I fix or pivot my brand?", "how do I move upmarket?"
**Core idea:** Four steps. (1) Pick who. (2) Find out what they like. (3) Associate yourself with those things deliberately and repeatedly. (4) Prune and adjust for profit. Then repeat every day, because new people arrive every day who don't know you.
**How to run it:**
1. **Who:** a market that's in pain, can afford it, is easy to target and is growing (playbook example: a business-law firm narrowing to assisted-living centre owners).
2. **What they like:** list their goals and pains (more customers, profit, a sellable business, recruiting and retaining staff, fewer lawsuits, lower taxes).
3. **Associate:** make content that solves those things (videos, articles, PDFs, guides), distribute it, iterate on the audience's response. Pair with people, products and outcomes they admire. Also make products (free and paid) they like, and appear next to people they like.
4. **Speed up trust with the Branding Cycle (authority levels):** what *we* say (content, ads, outreach: least influence) → what *others* say (customers, affiliates) → what *they experience* (most influence). Deliver on promises or the cycle runs in reverse.
5. **Brand goes both ways:** the brand sells the product; the product then rebrands the brand. Who recommends you also colours you (an endorser's identity transfers). Ads drive brand short-term; product experience drives it long-term.
6. **Generic → premium:** pair a commodity (a $10 hat) with what the audience admires and it becomes a premium item (his example: $89). Customers buy the product *and* the association ("now I am an ambitious entrepreneur"). It's profitable when the premium covers the cost of the pairings.
**Expansion — five directions (each needs new associations):** up-market (bigger customers), down-market (smaller), adjacent (similar buyer, different model), broader (whole category), narrower (a slice). **Bouquet metaphor:** a brand is a bouquet of associations. Narrowing = only red roses. Broadening = more flower types. Adjacent = red roses → yellow roses. Weeds (bad pairings) spoil the whole bouquet. He usually advises narrower first: more than half of portfolio companies got *more* specific, not broader, in that exercise (2024).
**Pivots are bets on net audience:** you'll lose some fans (the small-town band that "sold out"); make sure you gain more than you lose. Don't let five mean comments stop you gaining 500 new fans.
**Mistakes:** accidental brands (any podcast, any customer, any trend) leave people unsure what you stand for. When a bad pairing happens, don't try to erase it; overwhelm it with more of what your audience likes.
**Numbers & thresholds:** Branding outperforms direct response *over long horizons* only. His checklist closes by telling you to give it about five years and not quit. His content went broad, attracted "wantrepreneurs", and he re-centred on business owners.
**Ask the founder:** Which audience do you want to own in 5 years? What 3–5 things should people associate with you? What current pairing (topic, client type, partner) is a weed?
**Pairs with:** What a Brand Is; Content Strategy (narrow > wide); Lead Getters/affiliates (file 04).
**Sources:** $100M Playbook: Branding · YT "13 Years of Marketing Advice in 85 Mins" (2024)

**Hormozi's own clarity rules for copy (from sources):**
- Assume they don't know you, are in a rush, and read at a third-grade level. Edit until a readability checker says ~grade 3. Simple language doesn't attract dumber people; it makes it easier for everyone.
- Clear beats clever; deletion beats explanation.
- Specific about the pain, vaguer about the gain (compliance + believability).
- Data claims: % of people → outcome → timeframe → conditions (fewest conditions possible).
- Metaphors connect the unknown to something the audience knows (SEO is like an investment account, ads are like commission income).
- Give a reason ("because…"). Any reason beats none (the "can I cut in line because…" study he cites).
- Third-person / no guaranteed returns in regulated niches. Use compliance-audit firms if ads get flagged (he cites ~$5–6k audits with 90%+ post-audit approval for good firms; 2024).

## Procedure: Write Me 10 Hooks
**Use when:** the founder asks for hooks, headlines, opening lines, subject lines or thumbnail text.
**Step 0 — gather inputs (ask if missing; don't invent proof):**
- Avatar (who exactly; words they call themselves)
- Dream outcome + main pain, in their words
- Real proof: numbers, client results, credentials, timeframe (only what's true)
- Offer or CTA destination (lead magnet, call, product)
- Platform/format (ad, short, long video, email subject, LinkedIn post)
- Awareness level of the audience (default: mixed)
- Any past winning hooks (to reuse: they're the 70%)
**Step 1 — allocate (70-20-10):**
- **7 proven:** adapt the founder's past winners first. If none, adapt proven structures (the seven verbal types; the awareness-level templates; his formulas like "Read this if you…", "How to [result] without [pain]", "[N] ways [avatar] [result] without [common method]", "If you're [situation], [blunt consequence]").
- **2 winner-adjacent:** permutations of the best 7 (swap callout, number, timeframe, visual).
- **1 wild:** a pattern break (odd visual, meme, confession, contrarian claim) — must still be true.
**Step 2 — spread across awareness levels** (unless told otherwise): ~2 offer/proof (warm), ~4 promise/pain (solution/problem aware), ~2 curiosity (unaware), plus the 2 adjacent.
**Step 3 — write each hook with both parts:** a **callout** (label, situation or visual cue) + an **implied value** (what they get if they keep watching or reading).
**Step 4 — QA every hook:**
- Specific enough to qualify, broad enough to scale?
- Clear, not clever? Readable at ~grade 3? Under ~15 words spoken (~5 seconds)?
- True and provable? (State the facts. No invented numbers.)
- Matches what the body actually delivers? (Clear packaging beats bait.)
- Is the nonverbal hook specified for video? (Prop, setting, on-screen text.)
**Step 5 — output format:** a numbered table with columns **Hook · Type (label/question/conditional/command/statement/list/story/exclamation) · Awareness level · Bucket (70/20/10) · Visual/on-screen text (video only)**. Then one line: which 3 to test first, and the test plan (record all 10 against the same body; for long video, pick 3–4; for email, split-test 2–3 subject lines). Remind them to log results in the hook spreadsheet.

## Procedure: Ad Script Template (fill-in)
**Use when:** "write me an ad / ad script / video ad / Meta ad".
**Inputs to collect first:** avatar · awareness level · offer and price (or free lead magnet) · strongest *true* proof (numbers, named clients with permission) · guarantee/urgency/scarcity (if real) · landing step (what they see after clicking) · format choice (demo/testimonial/education/story/faceless) · length (15s / 30–60s / 2–4 min).

```
AD: [name] | Avatar: [ ] | Awareness: [unaware/problem/solution/product/most]
FORMAT (meat): [demonstration | testimonial | education | story | faceless]

1) CALLOUT (0–2s)
   Verbal: "[Avatar label / situation]…"
   Visual: [prop, setting, on-screen text that signals the avatar]

2) HOOK (2–5s) — record 10 variants; list them separately
   [Condition for value: "If you [situation], [what you'll get/learn]"]

3) MEAT (5–45s) — fulfils the hook at this awareness level
   Pain (specific, in their words): [ ]
   More bad / less good if they don't act: [ ]
   Mechanism / what we do differently (1–2 lines, metaphor if technical): [ ]
   PROOF (show > tell; raw > polished; looks like them):
     [% of clients] got [outcome] in [time] under [conditions]
     or: [client like them] went from [before #] to [after #] in [time]
   Offer: [what they get] + [bonus] + [guarantee if real]

4) CTA (last 5–10s) — clear > clever
   What/how: "[Tap the button below / comment X / call this number]"
   When/why now: "[deadline / limited slots, real capacity / any honest reason]"
   What they get: "[the free audit / guide / trial]"
   What happens next: "[you'll pick a time on the next page / it lands in your inbox]"
   Show it: [screen-record the click → form → thank-you/scheduler]

5) COMPLIANCE CHECK
   No guaranteed income/health claims · claims match proof · consent for testimonials
   · regulated niche → third-person framing / compliance review

VARIANTS PLAN: 10 hooks × this meat × 2 CTAs = 20 ads.
Winner → Kaleidoscope (background, re-record, reorder, filters, captions, pace, music).
```

**Worked mini-example (illustrative, 30s, faceless + testimonial):**
- Callout: "Founders of 20–100-person service companies…"
- Hook: "…your team spends a day a week copying data between tools. Here's what that costs."
- Meat: screenshot montage of a client Slack thread ("approvals now take 4 minutes"). On-screen: "Ops team: 22 hrs/week → 6 hrs/week in 45 days" (only if true and permitted). One line of mechanism: "We build agents on the tools you already use. No migration."
- CTA: "Tap below for a free 15-minute Ops Leak Scan. You'll see the 5 tasks to automate first. We only take 4 builds a month." Then show the scan page and the scheduler.
